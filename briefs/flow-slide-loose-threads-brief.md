**[F13] On a full deck the start costs three frames and the hand-back frames are back at their pre-fix size.** The user's quiet run, sixteen clicks between `tug/goodly-ferry` and `tug/goodly-treat`, on 21,007 elements, 1,259 stacking contexts and 6,394 render-layer candidates (Overview 7,379) — the size `[B04]` calls full. Which phase pays is the sampled reading's to say. **(verified, measured with the verb)**

**[F14] Sampled on the full deck, WebContent's main thread is mostly script, and most of that script runs in microtasks; selector queries from deck script are the largest named DOM cost.** The user's `--sample` run (22,012 elements, 6,470 candidates, sixteen clicks; raw reports kept under `$TMPDIR/tug-slide-sample-72371/`). Sampling was at 1 ms, which stopped WebContent so often that the run's own frame numbers are not a reading (first frame 177–416 ms against `[F13]`'s 80–88) — the verb now samples at 5 ms and says so beside any frame numbers taken under `--sample`. The shares survive the slowdown. WebContent main thread, 11,289 busy samples by deepest phase: script 41%, dom query 14%, style 11%, layout 9%, compositing 7%, rendering update 6%, layer commit 3%, paint 2%, gc 2%. By the task each sample ran in: microtasks 36%, the click task itself 23% (the verb dispatches the click from a zero timeout), the rendering update 17%, rAF / ResizeObserver / IntersectionObserver / event dispatch 11% together. The JavaScript is JIT code and has no symbols in a native sample — 2,249 unnamed script samples sit under a microtask drain — so which functions pay is not something `sample` can say. Selector queries are named by their bindings: `document.querySelector` inside the click task is the largest single source (365 samples), then `Element.querySelectorAll` in microtasks (119) and `document.querySelectorAll` in the click task (99). The GPU process's `RemoteRenderingBackend` work queue is the one busy thread there, almost entirely drawing. The `Tug` host is nearly idle. Also corrected in the verb: the lead block now counts the click's own task (the gap from the click to the first heartbeat), which every earlier reading — hand-rolled and `[F12]`/`[F13]` alike — left out, so those lead figures read low. **(verified, measured with the verb; the JavaScript attribution is not done)**

**[F15] Every lead-block figure before this one measured the heartbeat, not the main thread.** The zero-timer heartbeat on the release deck free-runs at 7–9 ms gaps at rest (measured: of 60 gaps over 400 ms, 46 were 7–9 ms), and every reading — hand-rolled and the verb's first version alike — counted any gap over 4 ms as wholly blocked. So the free-running heartbeat itself read as blocking, and the "215 ms of lead blocking" in `[F04]`, the 168–181 ms of `[F12]` and the 148–166 ms of `[F13]` are mostly that artefact; their *trend* across deck sizes is not evidence of anything. The verb now runs the heartbeat for 150 ms before the click, takes its median gap as the cadence, and counts only each gap's excess over it, from the click itself to 260 ms. The first reading with it, from a session mid-turn on a 10,392-element deck: 84–86 ms beyond an 8 ms cadence. The verb also gained `--queries`, which wraps `querySelector`, `querySelectorAll`, `closest` and `matches` for each click's window and reports every selector's calls, time and calling stack; on that same smoke run the focus engine's re-projection after an activation (`setKeyCard` → `reproject` → `applyProjection` → `computeProjection`) made about 25 `document.querySelector` calls by responder id per click, and selector queries came to about 23 ms of the lead. The page clock ticks in whole milliseconds, so a query's time is the share of its calls that crossed a tick — sound summed over many calls, meaningless for one. **(verified, measured; the smoke run is not a reading of the gesture)**

**[F16] A quarter to a third of the lead block is selector queries, and two engines make nearly all of them, by re-running whole-document or whole-card scans many times in one activation.** `--queries` over sixteen clicks on an 11,694-element deck (this session was mid-turn, but the query *counts* do not depend on that or on deck size): the lead is blocked 75–154 ms (typically ~80) beyond an 8–9 ms heartbeat, and selector queries take ~24 ms of it. Two sources: (1) **the focus engine projects about nine times per activation.** `FocusManager.reproject` is called synchronously from roughly fifteen state-change sites in `focus-manager.ts` (`setKeyCard`, key-view changes, default-ring registration, …), and every pass (`applyProjection`) runs three document-wide `querySelectorAll` scans (`[data-key-view]`, `[data-key-within]`, `[data-default-ring]`) to clear stale marks, plus `computeProjection`'s lookups — one responder looked up by `[data-responder-id="…"], [data-tug-focusable="…"]` ~24 times per click, and a `[data-tug-key-sink]` scan in `legalKeyboardElement`. (2) **the host-menu state flush asks the responder chain each action's state one by one** (`host-menu-state.ts` → `queryActionStateInKeyCard`), and each call runs `ResponderChain.findKeyCardContentId` (`responder-chain.ts`), which looks the key card up by `document.querySelector` and then scans the card's whole subtree with `querySelectorAll("[data-responder-id]")` to find its `card-content` node — ~33 times per click, recomputing the same answer every time. Both cost more as the deck and the focused card grow. The remaining two-thirds of the lead is not attributed; `[F14]`'s sample places most of it in script run from microtasks. **(verified, measured with the verb and read from the code)**

**[F17] After the responder-chain cache and a relaunch, the slide delivers its early frames again — but the relaunch is not separated from the fix.** Build `1156af5a0` (the cache in `ResponderChain.findKeyCardContentId`), freshly launched, 10,958 elements and 3,503 candidates, sixteen clicks, run from a session mid-turn: 7–11 frames in the first 200 ms (2–5 before, on an 11,694-element deck); the first two gaps ~24 and ~21 ms (~65 and ~95 before); the hand-back frame at ~440–470 ms gone in all sixteen (49–56 ms in every click before); lead blocked 69–116 ms beyond an 8 ms cadence (75–154 before); selector queries 19.5 ms per click (26.5 before), with `findKeyCardContentId`'s scans no longer among them. The cache's own share is small — the rows it removed came to ~5 ms per click — so it cannot account for ~70 ms of early gaps on its own. The other change is the relaunch: every earlier reading was on a WebContent process that had run since 08:26, whose `[F14]` sample carried real collector and heap-sweep cost. Separating them needs both conditions on fresh processes: a build with the cache reverted, launched fresh, read with the same verb — or this build read again after the deck has aged. **The A/B settled it: the relaunch, not the cache.** The same tree with the cache bypassed, freshly launched (11,304 elements, 3,615 candidates), read 6–10 frames in the first 200 ms, first gaps ~22 and ~18 ms, no hand-back frame in any of sixteen clicks, lead blocked 66–134 ms — the cached build's reading within noise — while selector queries rose back to 26.8 ms per click with the 64 subtree scans returned. So the cache is a real but small saving (~7 ms of queries per click, kept), and the frames came back with a fresh WebContent process. That reframes `[F12]`–`[F14]`: every reading that lost frames was taken on one process that had run since 08:26, and its element count grew with it — the transcripts and the Overview accumulate in a long-running page and a relaunch sheds them — so what `[F13]` read as deck size is at least partly process age. The open question becomes what a long-running deck accumulates that a fresh one does not; the aging re-read of this build answers whether it comes back, and when. **(verified, measured: A/B on fresh launches)**

## Decisions {#decisions}

**[B01] The remaining work is done on main, interactively, one item at a time.** The user's call: no arc wheel, no worktree, rebuild the Release app when a change needs to be on the deck to be read. Each item lands as its own commit when its reading or its pin says it is done. This rules out batching the items into one arc, and it is what makes the reading-driven items workable — each depends on the user rebuilding, relaunching and arranging the deck, which an unattended stage cannot do.

**[B02] The uncommitted brief edits land first.** The three files in `[F01]` carry the re-reading and the withdrawn premise. They are the record every item below cites, and a reading that lives only in a working tree is the kind of thing a relaunch or a stash loses.

**[B03] The first piece of work is a `tugtool deck motion slide` verb, and every reading after it is taken with it.** It drives the real gesture through the eval door — the Workspaces row click dispatched as pointer and click events, the same way the re-reading did so the numbers stay comparable — N times in each direction between two named cards, and reports per click and in aggregate: frames in the first 200 ms, the first frame's offset, the early gaps, every frame over one period with its time, main-thread blocking in the lead from a zero-timer heartbeat, and the settle mark's on and off times. Every report carries the deck's census (elements, stacking contexts, render-layer candidates, and the largest panes' element counts) beside its numbers, because `[F03]` showed a reading without it cannot be compared. It keys its window to a stamp it writes itself rather than to the stamp `gesture` waits for (`[F08]`). A `--tween` mode runs the bare-tween bisect (identity per pane, and a real move across the content panes, each read at its start and its end). A `--sample` mode runs `/usr/bin/sample` on the WebContent, GPU and `Tug` processes across the clicks and reports each thread's busy share and its heaviest frames, which closes `[F07]` without anyone reducing a sample by hand. `--json` emits the whole report. It lives beside the other `deck motion` verbs in `deck_motion.rs`, so it is part of the bundle and works on any instance. The argument: `[F09]` is why the work stalled, and every remaining item is either a reading or needs one to attribute it.

**[B04] Nothing is designed against `[F04]` or `[F05]` until the verb has attributed it on a full deck.** A full deck means one at the size the costs were found at — roughly 20,000 elements and 7,000 render-layer candidates, with a grown Overview — and the census in the verb's report is what shows whether a reading qualifies. This is the remaining-costs brief's `[B05]` and `[B08]` carried forward: fixes for this gesture designed against a bench, or against a deck that happened to be small, have missed before. The lead block is attributed first, because it is the larger number and has named suspects.

**[B05] The land-frame attribution is pursued only if the land frames come back on a full deck.** `[F06]` matters if there is a cost to prevent from returning. If the full-deck reading shows none at the hand-back, the three fixes are pinned already (`at0621`, `at0628`, `at0663`), and spending rebuilds to separate them buys nothing.

**[B06] The compositing-walk floor and the Overview's size go to the unification work and to the user, not to this work.** The walk (`[F10]` of the remaining-costs brief) is priced by how much is on the deck and is now recorded in `briefs/graphics-animations-asks.md` as the floor that work plans inside. Whether the Overview skips unseen rows or holds fewer of them is a product call the user owns. This work's verb measures both; it changes neither.

**[B07] The covers-check reds are fixed alongside the verb.** `[F11]`'s deck-canvas fan-out came in with the rail fix and is this work's to settle — narrow the `@covers` lines or record the coupling in `ACCEPTED_FANOUT` with its count. The themes width is not flow-slide work, but it is a one-line record change that would otherwise keep the check red under every commit this work makes.
