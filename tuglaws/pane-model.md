# Pane Model

*The canonical hierarchy for the tugdeck canvas: Deck → Pane → Card. This document formalizes what each concept is, what it owns, and the naming rules that encode it everywhere — code, CSS, DOM, wire format, and menus.*

---

## The Rule

**A Deck holds Panes; a Pane holds Cards; a Card holds one content component.** Position, size, z-order, drag, resize, and chrome are Pane responsibilities. Content identity — `componentId`, `title`, `state` — is a Card responsibility. Multi-card Panes surface a tab strip; a single-card Pane does not. Tabs are a UI affordance for switching among a Pane's Cards, not a separate data concept.

Everything else in this document explains how that rule manifests in code.

---

## Three Concepts

### Deck

The top-level canvas. Owns the layout tree: the set of all Panes, the ordered `cardIds` inside each Pane, and the currently-active Pane. A Deck is a flat state: `{ cards: Card[], panes: Pane[], activePaneId?: string, imposition? }` — not a tree. Cards and Panes live in two flat arrays, and every card belongs to exactly one Pane's `cardIds` list.

| Owner | Responsibility |
|-------|---------------|
| `DeckManager` | Mutations on the layout tree, invariant validation, subscription surface (`useSyncExternalStore`) |
| `DeckCanvas` | Renders the Panes, promotes the active Pane's active Card as first responder on mount |
| `DeckState` (type) | `{ cards, panes, activePaneId?, imposition? }` — the full shape of the tree |

**Deck invariants** (enforced by `validateDeckState`):
1. Every `pane.cardIds` entry references a real `state.cards[].id`.
2. Each card appears in exactly one pane's `cardIds` (no orphans, no duplicates).
3. No pane has `cardIds.length === 0` — closing the last card closes the Pane.
4. Every `pane.activeCardId` is a member of that Pane's `cardIds`.
5. `state.activePaneId`, when set, references a real Pane.
6. At most one Pane hosts each **sidebar** card's componentId, and that Pane carries no `slot` — a sidebar is the imposition's fixed end, not a link in its chain.

### Pane

The visual container. A rectangular frame on the canvas with chrome (title bar, optional tab bar, width and close controls) and a content region. Panes own **position**, **size**, **z-order** (implicit in array order within `deckState.panes`), **acceptsFamilies**, and the ordered `cardIds` list of the Cards they host.

| Owner | Responsibility |
|-------|---------------|
| `TugPane` (component) | Renders the frame; handles drag, resize, title bar, width popup, snap, tab bar |
| `TugPaneBanner` (component) | Renders the pane-scoped modal banner (error/status overlays) |
| `TugPaneState` (type) | `{ id, position, size, cardIds, activeCardId, title, acceptsFamilies, slot?, widthPreset? }` |

A Pane is a **responder** (per [L11]) for actions on Pane state: `close`, `find`, `toggleMenu`. A Pane is **not** responsible for Card content — it delegates to the active Card's `CardHost`.

#### Three geometry modes

A Pane always owns its geometry ([L09]); what varies is what it *derives* that geometry from. There are three modes, and they are mutually exclusive:

| Mode | Marked by | Horizontal | Vertical | Gestures |
|------|-----------|------------|----------|----------|
| **free** | no `slot`, and not a pinned sidebar | `position.x` / `size.width` | `position.y` / `size.height` | drag anywhere; all eight resize handles; snap |
| **pinned** | hosts a **sidebar-role** card whose `imposition.sidebars[id].pinned` is not `false` | held against the side its entry names, one gap in, its rail's width wide | one gap below the top, the deeper gap above the bottom — or its share of that run when it shares the rail | draggable — and the drag is what unpins it; deck-facing edge resize only (width), which keeps the pin |
| **imposed** | `slot: number` | pinned to the slot's anchor across the layout span | same vertical run as pinned | drag or resize evicts it back to free |

Both derived modes are the layout imposer's (`lib/layout-imposer.ts`), and the difference between them is what the arrangement's *end* is versus what its *slots* are.

**Pinned is a sidebar card.** It is not marked by a field on the Pane: a sidebar Pane is one hosting a card whose registration declares `layoutRole: "sidebar"` ([D128] — jots, overview, tripwires, arcs, cards and layout today; the arcs card's component id is the durable `dashes`), derived by `findSidebarPanes`, and whether it is standing at its pin is the deck-level `imposition.sidebars[componentId].pinned` (absent reads as pinned). It holds the arrangement's fixed end at a constant pin and keeps its rail's width, so it never overlaps — which is the point, because a slotted card can end up overlapped and a rail must never be slid under. Its side is that entry's `side`, one of the deck's layout axes.

**Both edges can carry a rail, and one edge can carry two cards.** Cards sharing a side share one rail, and **how tall each of them stands is the user's to set, per side.** The record is `imposition.rails[side]` — `{ order?, shares? }`, absent reading as an equal division — and it is written by a seam drag, by *Resize Sidebars to Fit*, and by a title-bar drag through the drop-zone engine; the width model, the allocator, and every horizontal pin are untouched by it. **A rail is always divided ([D183]); the stack was retired with the choice.**

**The members divide the rail's vertical run and every one of them is visible.** Rails hold a z-band of their own, above every free Pane (a rail is never occluded by a card) and below the overlay base (or the rail would bury its own menus); within that band the members take the deck's ordinary array order, so `activateCard` raises one over the other — which decides nothing about what is on screen, since a divided rail draws every member at its own tile. A rail of ONE renders that member across the whole run, which is the same geometry an undivided rail always had.

**The division is CSS:** per-side seam custom properties (`--tug-rail-<side>-seam-<j>`, plain numbers in (0,1)) written on the frames' containing block by the same `deck-canvas.tsx` layout effect that writes the rail widths, and each member's `top`/`bottom` is a `calc()` over the seam either side of it. So a window resize re-resolves a split rail in the browser's own reflow and runs no JavaScript at all — the same reason the rail's width is a property rather than a length ([L06]). The run's endpoints are the pins an unsplit rail has, to the pixel, and the air between two members is one imposition gap, half from each. A **seam element** sits in that gap carrying `RAIL_SEAM_ZINDEX` (level with the frontmost rail rank, strictly below the overlay base): dragging it moves the boundary live and commits height weights. Its hit strip is wider than the 5px gap and therefore takes about 2.5px off each neighbour — a deliberate trade, since a 5px drag target is under every pointing-comfort floor the deck holds.

**Vertical order and heights are real, persisted state — and neither follows z-order.** `order` is a list of componentIds; `shares` is a weight per componentId, so a member that closes and returns keeps its height and a reorder moves cards without moving heights. The effective order is the stored one filtered to the members actually standing, then any unnamed ones in **registration** order — never the panes array, which `activateCard` reorders. A side's `order` materializes the moment a second card joins it, so a divided rail's vertical order is stored from the first frame rather than derived from anything that moves. Without both of those a click on the lower member would raise it, change the derived order, and visibly swap the two cards.

**A member arriving or leaving a place is a fade plus one true height change, never a stretch and never a slide** ([D135]). The settle's transform tween may carry a scale only within a capped raster distortion (`MAX_FLIP_SCALE_DISTORTION`), and a rail gaining or losing a member halves or doubles a surviving member's height, which no cap admits. So the member that survives the flip on screen crosses by a real `height` term, its subtree laying out truthfully on every frame, through TugAnimator and never rAF ([L13]). **Its move and its height ride one effect**, because the edge it keeps is pinned by the two cancelling — a member growing into the run from the bottom tile translates up by exactly the height it gains — and two effects would put the transform on the compositor and the height on the main thread and let that edge slide. **Every other member fades and does not move at all**, concurrently in the same settle window: a member arriving at a tile it has never held, or leaving for a place it will not be seen, has no journey worth animating. The survivor is the **z-frontmost** member, because that is the card a column's stack shows — grow the top tile instead and the growth belongs to a frame that ends up hidden while the card the user is left looking at arrives by a cut. It is therefore not always at the top of the run, and a frontmost bottom member travels up as it grows; with no scale in play that travel is a true translate and costs nothing. Width presets past the cap cross by a real `width` tween the same way; only the adjacent step still rides `scaleX`.

**Membership churn never destroys the arrangement** ([L23] — a card closing is an internal operation). A side that drops to one member renders that member across the whole run with order and shares untouched; when the member returns, the arrangement re-applies at the order and heights it had. Nothing anywhere deletes a rail arrangement — the record outlives its members exactly as `order` does, and a card that comes back to a rail it has a share in stands at that share.

**Three arrangements have now been lived on, and the survivor is the plainest.** An automatic vertical split shipped first and was rejected — it spent a rail to show two half-cards, which is what a card with two stacked sections was already doing inside one rail, only less space-efficient. The occlusion stack that replaced it was lived on and found wanting the other way: it hides content the user wants visible at once. [D134]'s synthesis, that the division is a per-side choice among Stack, Fit and Flow, was lived on longest and cost the most: three modes, a measurement pipeline behind them, and a seed that moved the user's own sashes whenever a card came or went. [D183] is the verdict on all three — a rail is always divided, its sashes are the hand's, and the one algorithm left runs when the user asks for it.

**The factory rail is the factory deck.** A launch that finds no persisted layout at all builds the default one empty, and the first content card the user opens stands the whole rail with it — `claimFactoryRail` pins Cards, Arcs, Layout and Tripwires at `DEFAULT_SIDEBAR_SIDE` (right) in one commit, stacked in that order with Cards frontmost — so the first deck a user ever meets, the one setup hands them, has its tools on it. Only a genuinely absent layout takes that path: a deck the user has emptied is a layout that says empty, and it is left as they left it.

**A pinned sidebar card is still draggable, and dragging it by the title bar is the only way off the pin.** The commit carries the same `evictSlot` an imposed pane's drag carries — one gesture, one rule: a manual move releases a Pane from whatever was deriving its geometry. Off the pin the card is an ordinary free Pane (eight handles, snap, tiling) and the arrangement spans the whole canvas, exactly as it does when the card is closed. Any choice on the **Layout** card pins it back, as does closing and reopening it. Widening it by its deck-facing edge does **not** unpin it — that gesture commits width alone, and the arrangement keeps laying out against the moving edge live.

**A pinned sidebar card can also be carried to the other rail, or to the empty edge, without leaving the pin.** A rail card's drag vocabulary is both rails ([B08] of the rail-refinements brief): its own rail advertises N positions — it is already one of them — and the other rail advertises N + 1, because there it is an arrival. Content cards still never see a rail and a rail card still never sees a slot. What the other side offers depends on what stands there. A rail holding members offers its positions, division-true, and a foreign card held at an overflowing rail's edge scrolls that strip exactly as one of its own members would, because the autoscroll asks by rail rather than by the card's side — and it asks over a band read off a member that stayed put, so a card carried across the deck does not take its old rail's band with it. A rail holding ONE member offers itself whole — one zone, the rail's one rect — since a lone member takes the run undivided and there are no positions to divide it into; the arrival lands at index 0 of the stored order and the two divide from there. A side with **no rail** holds a landing strip open while a rail card is in the air: a `.tug-rail-vacancy` tile at the rail's anchor and the dragged card's own rail width, inert to the pointer, shown as a hairline under the canvas's `data-carrying`, and read by `enumerate` as that side's one position — the third place the vacancy pattern applies, after the deck's empty slots and for the same reason (the tile is the promise the indicator draws). Every landing is **one commit**: `moveSidebarToRail` composes the card's side and both rails' orders into a single imposition on the split's own precedent, so the gesture arms one settle and the card lands where the outline said rather than at the side's default position first. And every place a drag chooses between is measured from **seated** frames only — the run from the deck's own measurement, the strip from a member that is not moving, and the dragged card's own gesture-start rect only when it is its rail's sole member — so the outline never follows the hand.

**Imposed is a slot.** The deck's `imposition.kind` (`"one-up"` through `"six-up"`) defines numbered slots across the span — the canvas minus each side's rail width and one gap. A slot is an anchor at a fixed fraction of the band: a Pane of width *w* in slot *k* of *N* sits `k / (N − 1) × max(0, band − w)` from the band's left edge. Slot 0 hugs that edge, the last slot hugs the band's right, and the ones between space evenly. **Numbering always runs left to right** — slot 1 is the leftmost position on the deck, whatever side the rail holds. A rail's side moves the band's edges, never the numbering; a number that meant "left" on one deck and "right" on another would be a number you had to think about before you could use it. It also keeps the pin's *shape* the same on both decks, which is what a side flip has to interpolate.

The property that rule exists for: **a Pane's place depends on its own width and nothing else's.** No slotted Pane can see another, so closing, opening, widening, or restacking any card leaves every other card exactly where it was. A slot is a place in the arrangement, never a place in a queue. Slack therefore spreads evenly between cards rather than pooling in one margin; an arrangement that holds still is worth more than one whose margins collect in one place.

**A slot resolves two ways, and which one is the deck's to choose.** `imposition.layout` is `"fit"` or `"flow"` (absent reads as **flow**, which is what a deck that has never chosen stands in — a deck should not take width away from a card the reader chose until the reader asks it to), written by the Layout card's `set-imposition-layout` action. **Fit** is the travel-fraction rule above. **Flow** reads the same slots as ordinal positions in a strip: the slots stand side by side in index order, each taking its own extent — its widest member's width — one imposition gap apart, so `stripLeft(k)` is the running sum of everything before it. **Every slot the kind defines holds its place, occupied or not.** An empty one reserves `vacancyExtent` — the widest card standing in the chain, or the deck's content width when nothing does — so slot 3 standing empty holds a card's width of strip open and a card assigned to slot 4 stands at slot 4. Flow read the strip as a sequence of occupied slots once, and the arrangement collapsed under its own numbering: a deck with cards in slots 1, 2 and 4 drew itself `1|2|4`, and a chord naming slot 4 moved its card nowhere the eye could follow, because that card's place was already the third position in the run. Holding the empty ones open is also what puts the two modes in step, since a fit anchor is a travel fraction and an empty slot has always kept its share of the band; flow was the outlier. `kind` still names how many slots there are and still answers ⌘1..9 and the miniature; only what a slot MEANS changes.

**The two modes trade the same quantity in opposite directions.** Fit spends crowding on **overlap** and keeps the property that a Pane's place depends on its own width alone. Flow spends it on the **right edge** and keeps the property that no two occupied slots ever overlap — and pays for that by making a Pane's place depend on its neighbours' widths, which is the invariant `ImposedPlacement` names outright. Flow pays it in exactly one place: the strip is resolved once per commit in the deck canvas's placements memo (`deckFlowStrip`) and handed down; no Pane resolves a strip from its own state. Stacking within a slot is unchanged — flow removes collisions *between* slots, not within one.

**Both rules are one resolution, and every reader takes it from there.** `stripPositions(layout, occupied, {band})` dispatches: `flowStripPositions` sums the extents, `fitStripPositions` applies the travel fraction across the band, and both answer in the same shape and the same coordinates — origin at the band's near edge, one extent per slot, and a `width` for how much room the arrangement takes. In fit that width IS the band, which is the same statement as "fit has nothing to scroll". `deckSlotStrip(state, band)` is the deck's reading of it, layout-blind; `deckFlowStrip(state)` is the flow-only face the canvas and the reveal already use. **Anything that draws the arrangement must come through here rather than derive a second rule.** The Layout card's plan derived its own fit layout once — N equal blocks tiling the field, on the belief that fit normalizes its cards to fill the band — and it does not: the picture then showed neither the air a wide band leaves nor the lap a narrow one forces, and it jumped whenever the layout toggled even though the deck had barely moved.

**The strip is revealed, not scrolled.** A flow deck longer than its band carries a **viewport offset** — one number on `DeckState`, session-only, absent reading as 0 — written by exactly one rule: on activation, the minimal change that brings the active Pane fully inside the band (`scrollRectToVisible` semantics; a Pane wider than the band pins its left edge instead). An activation that reveals nothing writes nothing, so a click on a visible card commits no geometry. The offset is not persisted because it is derivable — activating any card re-reveals it — and a restored one would be a viewport nobody asked for onto an arrangement that may have changed. It is applied as **one subtraction term in the same `left` calc** every imposed Pane already has, over two custom properties (`--tug-imposer-flow-offset`, `--tug-imposer-flow-strip`) written beside the rail insets, with its clamp expressed in CSS — so a window resize re-resolves flow in the browser's own reflow exactly as it re-resolves fit, with no JavaScript in the loop. **This is not a scroller and must never become one**: nothing here creates a scroll container, and the deck's "placed, never scrolled" rule below holds in flow unchanged — the strip is *placed* at an offset, the way every other imposed frame is placed.

**The mode is a term in the arrangement signature.** A fit↔flow toggle moves every Pane's `left` while kind, slots, widths, rails, and bullseye all hold still, and at rest the offset has not moved either — so without the mode as its own signature term the settle would not arm and the one gesture the mode exists to offer would be the one that cuts ([D135]). Bullseye supersedes the mode as it supersedes everything else — the bullseyed Pane is still centred one-up and takes no offset — but the line the *other* Panes sort around on their way out is resolved through the same `imposeStyle().left`, so on a flow deck that line is the flow left or they cross.

**The imposer never touches a Pane's width**: a slot is a position anchor, not a rect, so widths stay the user's and overlap is ordinary geometry rather than a case to handle. Any number of Panes may hold the same slot — a slot is a vertical stack whose top Pane is visible, and the Cards card's list is one switching surface.

**Width is chosen, not derived.** A content Pane's width comes from the user through one of four doors — a resize handle, the title-bar width popup, the ⌃⌘1/2/3 chords and their Window-menu twins ([D130]), or the deck-wide **Card Width** default — and every one but the drag writes it through the same `movePane` a drag uses, held between the Pane's stack floor and ceiling and stamped as `widthPreset` so a picker can show which named width it is at ([D128]). A manual resize clears the stamp: a settled control shows what the geometry holds, never what it was last told. The imposer sees none of this; it places whatever width it finds.

**The other switching surface is local, and lives in the title bar.** A Pane whose slot holds more than one renders a **stack badge** — a layers glyph and the slot's depth — at the head of its title-bar controls, so the deck admits at rest that something stands behind the card in front. Clicking it opens the **stack picker**, a menu whose rows are miniatures of the title bars they stand for — each Pane's own icon and its own title-bar text, topmost-first, with the front one check-marked — and the badge stays **lit** for as long as the menu is down, the way a macOS menu-bar title holds its highlight; **Cmd-clicking the title bar** opens the same picker without raising the Pane clicked (the meta modifier's existing "interact with a background window without raising it" reading, inherited rather than carved out); and **⌘R** opens it for the focused Pane and closes it again on a second press. **The stack's no-look motion is the depth pair** ([D129]): `Window ▸ Next Card in Stack` (⌥⌘]) brings the Pane buried longest straight to the front and puts nothing on screen at all, which is what lets the press be repeated without looking, and `Window ▸ Previous Card in Stack` (⌥⌘[) is its exact inverse — the front Pane goes all the way to the back (`sendPaneBehind`) and the one beneath it fronts. NEXT raises the **bottom-most** Pane rather than the one below the front, and that is the whole of why it is a ring: each raise sends the outgoing front Pane exactly one place back, so a depth-N slot is home again after N presses and the user can count instead of look. Raising the next-one-down instead would ping-pong between the top two forever and never reach the third, because each raise rewrites the order the "next" was computed from — which is also why PREVIOUS must be a true rotation rather than a raise of the second-from-top. It is also what ⌘-backtick does with windows. All three items gate identically (depth > 1); they are menu items and not keybinding-map entries because AppKit resolves key equivalents before the web view sees a keydown. Choosing a row raises that Pane through the same `transferFocusForActivation` a Cards row click or a ⌘N assignment takes: one gesture vocabulary, three doors. The badge shows on **every** Pane in the stack, not just the top one, because it describes the *place* and Panes in one place may differ in width — a peeking buried Pane's title bar would otherwise be the one that lied about where it stands; a fully covered Pane is `visibility: hidden` anyway, badge included, so a same-width **stack** shows exactly one. **A split rail shows one per member**, since nothing there is occluded: two badges telling the same true thing about the one rail they share, which is the honest reading rather than a duplicate.

**A slot divides too, and it is the same arrangement over the other kind of place.** `imposition.columns` is a `Record<slot, {mode, order, shares}>` — the exact shape `imposition.rails` holds per side — so the seam fractions, the seam handle, the member pins, and the split/stack choreography are one implementation serving two places rather than two that resemble each other. Stacked (the default, and what a slot was before this existed) the Panes sharing a slot draw the same rect and z-order decides which you see; split, the slot's vertical run is divided at `--tug-slot-<k>-seam-<j>` and every member is visible at once. **A split divides the RUN, never the band**: the members keep the slot's anchor and its width, and the first and last pin to the run's own endpoints rather than to fractions of it, so a column of one is byte-identical to a slot that was never split and the eye reads a split as a division of the card it already knew.

**A column stops dividing when its members' floors stop fitting.** Division is useful while every member can stand at the height it says it cannot go below; past that it produces slivers, and every seam drag makes the problem worse rather than better. So the standing is decided from the floors against the run — `Σ floor + (n − 1) · seam > run` — and a column that fails it takes a different geometry entirely: every member stands at `max(floor, natural · weight)`, the height IT asked for rather than a fraction of a run it no longer fits in, and they stack down a virtual strip with one imposition gap between them, which slides up behind the run on a per-slot `--tug-slot-<k>-column-offset`. The strip is longer than the run and **the card the run's bottom edge cuts IS the affordance** — the vertical twin of flow's card half-hidden at the band edge, and read the same way. Its coordinates are published as `--tug-slot-<k>-strip-<j>`, `n + 1` of them for `n` members: every member's top, then the strip's own end. The offset is clamped in CSS against that last coordinate less the run, so a window resize re-resolves the column in the browser's own reflow ([L06]); the stored number is re-clamped afterwards only so the next reveal computes from an offset the deck is actually showing. Activating a member below the run slides the strip by the least that shows it, inside the same commit that raises it, so raise and slide are one arrangement change — the same shared, axis-free reveal rule flow uses over widths.

**A card is dragged to a place, not to a coordinate.** Dragging an arrangeable card by its title bar moves it freely under the pointer, and throughout the gesture exactly one advertised **drop zone** is indicated — initially the card's own current position, which is what makes a zero-travel release a no-op rather than a question. The indication snaps between zones as the pointer moves, and the release animates the card into the zone that was showing and commits that zone's mutation. Because a zone is always live, an unmodified release is always meaningful: there is no "released over nothing" to invent an answer for. A zone's rect is the tile the card would occupy if released there, derived under the standing the target column would have AFTER the drop and with the commit's own seam arithmetic: for each candidate position the post-drop order — sitting members with the dragged card inserted there — runs through `allocatePlaceHeights`, floors and weights travelling with their cards and an arrival weighing 1, and the tile is that allocation's cut of the run. So a two-member column about to take a third whose floors no longer fit draws the strip those floors force rather than its shares, a one-member column about to take a second draws the two halves the created split's seam will divide at — never the arrival stacked below the member's current height and hanging off the run — and a member reordering a shares-bearing place previews its own share standing wherever it lands. Tile and landing come from the same pure function, so the indicator cannot promise a geometry the commit will not produce.

**A zone is asked for at the tile the indicator draws for it.** The indicator is a preview of where the release lands, so the region that selects a position is the region the preview draws: the drag latches at the title bar, the pointer IS the title bar, and the moment it crosses into the next drawn tile the indication follows. Concretely, a stacked place's positions are asked for over full-run bands divided at each successive tile's top edge, the outermost stretched to whichever is further out, the run's edge or the strip's own, so a column scrolled off its run still has every position askable somewhere. Dividing anywhere else — the sitting members' midpoints were the previous rule — makes the indication contradict its own drawing: the title bar stands inside the bottom tile while the outline still claims the top one, and on a tall member the switch lands three quarters of the way down the run instead of where the preview said it would. Rails divide the same way, for the same reason. What keeps every position reachable is that the bands, unlike the tiles, always abut into a full cover of the run — a hanging overflow tile's band stretches to that tile's own bottom edge rather than pinching to a sliver.

**Containment outranks distance, and center distance only breaks ties.** A pointer standing inside a zone's band is asking for that zone whatever the arithmetic says about anywhere else; a tall slot's center is far from its own top edge, so scoring by center alone puts a pointer resting on a card's title bar nearer the *middle* of a neighbouring column than the slot it is physically inside. Center distance still separates the one nesting the vocabulary guarantees — a tab bar always lies within the band of the pane it belongs to — where the thin bar's center is close and the tall band's is far.**The vocabulary depends on what kind of card is moving, and the two do not meet.** A content card's zones are the positions of its own split column, every insertion position of another split column, the two dividing positions of a slot where one foreign pane stands alone — **body-drop divides**: committing either creates the split, upper half index 0 and lower half index 1, while stacking onto that card remains the tab-bar gesture — every stacked multi-pane slot as a whole (a stack is an arrangement the user chose, and body-dropping into it joins it), every unoccupied slot anchor in either mode, and every other pane's tab bar — the last being the one drop zone the app has always had, absorbed rather than orphaned. **An empty slot advertises because it is drawn.** The engine is handed rects, never a solve, and a slot with no pane in it has nothing to measure — so the empty anchor was in the vocabulary from the first commit and unreachable the whole time. Every held-open slot now paints a **vacancy tile**: a pointer-inert box at the anchor and the reserved width, positioned by the same `imposeStyle` expression a Pane takes, holding a numbered `TugSlot` badge centered in both axes. The box is the measurement and the badge is the picture, and they are deliberately different sizes — the first cut outlined the whole reserved room in a dim hairline and read as a rendering fault rather than as a statement, because a several-hundred-pixel empty rectangle says nothing and an empty place has exactly one short thing to say: *this is place four, and there is no card in it.* The badge says it in the vocabulary the strip and the masthead already name places with. **The badge is painted only when a place is the subject of something** — a card in the air, or the deck answering a gesture that named this place — and at rest the tile draws nothing at all. Those are two different jobs: the box is a measurement that has to exist on every frame, while the drawing inside it is the reader's, and the reader wants it only in the moments where *which slot is this?* is the live question. A permanent numbered chip standing in an empty room reads as a CONTROL — border, fill and a numeral, exactly what every other chip in this vocabulary is — so the eye kept arriving at it expecting something to press, and it was the loudest thing on the one part of the deck where nothing was happening. The reveal is `opacity`, never `display`, because the flash animates the badge's own box shadow and it has to be laid out before the class arrives; the drag half reads `data-carrying`, written on the canvas by `gaugeDragFrame` — the drag's own lifecycle verb, so the affordance is tied to the gesture rather than to a hover or a store flag. **The flash half is not a second rule beside the animation — the badge's opacity rides the flash's own keyframes**, appearing on the first and gone by the last. The ring is drawn *on* the badge, so the two are one appearance and one departure; held apart, the ring would fade out softly and the badge would then snap off whenever its class was dropped, a hard edit landing after the soft one the eye was already following. `animation-fill-mode` stays `none`, so the run ends into exactly the state the rules declare and the class can be removed late without showing anything. Pinned by `at0465` and `at0466`. It is a drawing the reader can see a place in, and it is the rect the engine measures, which is why it is an element rather than an arithmetic answer — the alternative was re-deriving the anchor from the kind, the rail widths and the band, a second copy of geometry the CSS `calc()` chain owns. A pinned sidebar card's zones are its own rail's positions and nothing else. Rails are not zones for content cards and content slots are not zones for sidebar cards; cross-place drops are a later feature, and what keeps them later is that neither vocabulary can name the other's places. **One engine serves both** — the rail reorder's corridor, a horizontal band the pointer had to stay inside or the drag became a free one, is gone, and with it the second drag grammar.

**⌘ is the way out, and the only way out.** Holding ⌘ at the release suppresses zone indication entirely and drops the card at free pixels — evicting an imposed content card from its slot, or unpinning a sidebar card from its rail. The modifier is read up to the last instant rather than latched at the press, so a ⌘ tapped and released mid-drag is not a decision the user cannot take back. Without it the gesture is purely zone-based and never evicts, which is what the corridor used to do by accident: wandering far enough sideways was not a statement, and it should never have meant one.

**A drag has two endings that commit nothing, and both are real.** Escape cancels — the card animates home, the indicator goes away, and the arrangement is exactly what it was. `pointercancel` (a system gesture, a scroll takeover) does the same. Escape is registered on **window in capture** and swallowed there (`preventDefault` + `stopImmediatePropagation`), because the responder chain's own key listener is also a window-capture listener and would otherwise never let the key through — and because an Escape that both cancelled a drag and collapsed the Cards card's selection would be one keypress doing two unrelated things. **Refusal is the third ending, and it is visible**: a commit the store declines animates the card home and flashes its pane, never leaves the frame parked at a zone that did not take it.


**Every drop animates into its zone, including the one that commits nothing.** The frame keeps the transform the hand left it on across the commit, and a FLIP carries it from the release rect into the place the commit gave it — measured, not predicted, so the landing starts exactly where the eye last saw the card. A release back onto the card's own position commits nothing and therefore renders nothing, and a refused commit changes nothing either; both still travelled under the hand, and both animate home by that same landing rather than snapping. The dragged frame keeps `data-pointer-owned` for the whole crossing, which is what holds the imposer's settle off it: the settle carries every *other* frame the commit moved, and this one is being carried by its own landing. Both run on `--tugx-imposer-settle-duration`, from `IMPOSITION_SETTLE_MS` — **the one knob for how fast cards move**, read resolved by `readSettleMs` at both call sites so a drop can never be the exception to it.
**Autoscroll moves the view during the gesture and commits it once at the end.** While the pointer holds within the autoscroll margin of an overflowing column's run edge — or the band's edge on a flow deck — the relevant offset advances at a constant rate on the gesture's own rAF ([D135]: no second clock), and the zones re-derive as it moves. **During the gesture the offset is written to its custom property and nowhere near the store.** The offset is an `arrangementSignature` term, so a per-frame commit would arm a FLIP settle on every frame of the drag; the dragged frame is exempt for carrying `data-pointer-owned`, but every other member of that column would be measured and tweened under the user's hand. The number commits once, at the drop — and also at a cancel, because a cancel takes back the move and not the view: the card goes home, and the place the user scrolled to is where they are still looking.

**An instrument away from the canvas reads the deck's motion through the gauge channel.** [L06] keeps per-frame motion out of React, which by construction leaves anything outside the canvas's own subtree — the Layout card's miniature, the flow strip — blind to it: the numbers reach only the elements the canvas writes custom properties onto. `tugdeck/src/lib/imposer-gauges.ts` extends that same mechanism across subtrees. An instrument **registers** an element for a named signal (`flow-offset`, `column-offset:<slot>`, `drag-frame`, `drag-zone`) in a layout effect ([L03]) and releases it with that effect; the canvas's existing per-frame writers **publish**, and the publisher sets the signal's custom properties on every registered element. There is no store, no state, and no render in the path, and a publisher nobody is registered with takes a fast path out. **An instrument may also register a listener rather than an element**, and that is not a convenience: a consumer whose live state is a THRESHOLD over the published value — "is this slot inside the band right now?" — cannot be served by a custom property at all, because CSS has no comparison that yields a value an emphasis system can read. Such a consumer takes the published map in JS, derives its discrete state, and projects it onto the DOM itself. A listener is primed with the standing value exactly as a late element is, fires after the elements are written, and counts toward the fast path — a signal nothing draws in CSS but something derives in JS must still fire. **Every value is a unitless fraction** — an offset as a fraction of the band or run it slides under, a rect as a fraction of the canvas box — because a consumer drawing at another scale cannot convert a pixel it is handed: CSS has no way to divide a length by a length, while a fraction multiplies straight into whatever unit the consumer draws in. Committed values keep flowing through the store, so a gauge carries only the motion **between** commits and its consumers compose `var(--gauge-…, <committed fallback>)`; the miniature writes that composition as a translation away from its committed pins, which is what lets a drawing at rest need no publisher at all. Two attributes ride the same channel — `data-gauge-drag` while a drag is live and `data-gauge-zone` while it is offering a place — so drag-only affordances gate in pure CSS, and a ⌘-freed stretch keeps the ghost while dropping the highlight exactly as the canvas's own indication does.

**In flow the deck says where it stands, in the band it already keeps.** A flow strip runs past the band it is seen through, and a deck showing one slice of its chain owes the reader a statement of which slice. The **flow strip** furnishes the bottom imposition gap — the deeper one, reserved so nothing imposed collides with the host's build stamps — with the arrangement **drawn to scale**: one segment per slot the kind defines, each at its own place along the strip and at its own width, drawn as the same `TugSlot` the Cards card's slot picker arranges slots with, **centered** on the canvas. Every slot is drawn, occupied or not, because every slot holds its place; an empty one is a place waiting for a card and is drawn the size of the cards it stands among. **What is on screen is said by the band, twice and continuously**: as a bracket drawn over the strip, and as a pair of **veils** dimming toward the canvas everything the band does not show. Both are `calc()` over the same two numbers, so they cannot disagree, and a segment half inside the band is half veiled — the truth a per-segment threshold could not tell. The bracket alone was tried first and was not enough: a hairline has to be traced before it can be read, and the fact a reader wants at a glance is simply *which part of this is the part I am looking at*, which brightness answers without being read at all. What stays forbidden is the per-segment register, which is where the accent would go — **where the reader is LOOKING and which card the reader is IN are different facts.** The first is the band, and it is neutral ink end to end, because a viewport is not a selection. The second is the reader's own card, marked at its slot, and it takes the accent, because a live selection is exactly what the accent is for. The neutral ink is the CARD pairing rather than the control one, because the strip stands on the deck canvas — a dark ground in every theme, the light ones included — where control ink authored for a light control surface simply vanishes. **The strip stands whenever the layout is flow, and overflow changes its register rather than its existence**: a strip inside its band is drawn quiet with the bracket withheld, and a strip that overflows raises every ink a step and reveals it. No element arrives or departs across that boundary, because a component that materialised when the strip grew would read as a new thing rather than as the same instrument speaking up. **Centering is what keeps it clear of the host's stamps** — a drawing bounded to a share of the canvas and centered on it cannot reach a corner — so the clearance is a property of the arrangement rather than a number somebody stated. Structure renders from the deck snapshot ([L02]); where the band stands *between* commits rides the gauge channel with no JS in the loop at all — the root registers as an element and the bracket's own `left` and `width` are a `calc()` over the published fraction, which is only possible because a bracket is a continuous quantity. The strip drops its pointer events while a zone drag is live, through the `data-gauge-drag` the channel already stamps on everything registered to it, so it can never shadow a hit band or swallow a release.

**Three gestures move the strip, and all three are one path.** A **scrub** across the strip, one of its segments clicked, and a sideways wheel on the canvas — a horizontal wheel, or shift with a vertical one — all write the offset per frame and commit exactly once, the same one-write-at-the-end rule the autoscroll obeys. Each clamps every frame with the store's own arithmetic, so the deck never draws a position the commit would refuse. A chip commits the offset that puts its slot **in the middle of the band**, and lets the settle animate the crossing; a scrub previews that same answer for every chip it crosses and commits the last one at release.

**Naming a place centers it; being taken to a place reveals it.** The two rules are deliberately different and the difference is who spoke. An ACTIVATION reveals — the minimal move that brings the card fully in, and no move at all when it is already there — because the reader asked for a card and the deck owes them the least disturbance that shows it. Except the pointer's: a press on a member clipped at a strip's edge activates it and moves NOTHING, because the hand is already on the card it named, and a strip that slid under the press would carry the title bar out from under a mouse still holding it — the one thing a press must never do. The pointer path passes `reveal: false` to `activateCard`, and then a release that travelled under the click threshold — a CLICK — calls `revealCard`, so a card the reader merely clicked still comes fully in, once the hand has let go; a release that travelled is a drag, and the drop answers for itself. That reveal CROSSES like every other arrangement change, which turns on a distinction the frame's marks must keep: `data-gesture` says a hand is on the frame (the lifted livery, the suspended height transition) and goes on at the press, while `data-pointer-owned` says the frame positions itself and goes on only when the drag latches. The settle and the cut detector read the second, never the first — a press that never travels leaves the frame the imposer's to carry, and a commit landing inside that press would otherwise cut the one card the reader clicked while every neighbour crossed. The keyboard, a chord, and a programmatic raise reveal at the activation as before, since there the reader is being taken somewhere they may not be looking. A reader who points at a segment, or types ⌃⌘*n*, named a PLACE, and the answer to a named place must not depend on where the band happened to be standing: under the minimal rule a slot merely visible at the band's edge would stay at the edge, so the same gesture would move the deck or not for reasons the reader cannot see. Centering has no offset in its inputs at all, which is the property stated as arithmetic. The clamp still pins the strip's two ends flush, so the gesture gives back less travel near an end and none at the very end — the correct answer there rather than an exception to the rule. **Which is why the verb is named for the arrival and not the geometry**: the row is *Go to Slot N*, because a row labelled *Center Slot 1* cannot do what it says, and a reader who watched slot 1 land flush at the left edge would reasonably conclude it was broken. The function stays `stripCenterOffset` — naming a rule for its arithmetic and a menu row for its outcome are different jobs. **The keyboard's arrival is answered with a flash**, the pane's accent ring or the vacancy badge's: the band moving is the only thing this verb does, and on a deck of similar cards that is not enough to say which one the reader asked for. The pointer's is not, because the hand is already on the segment it named. The scrub moves in whole slots and that is chosen rather than tolerated: a preview writes the offset directly and arms no settle, so there is no animator in the path ([L13]), and a paging control paging under the user's own finger is the idiom. The wheel is the one gesture with no release to commit on, so quiet is its ending: deliveries accumulate in a ref and a short idle timeout closes the gesture. It declines when the wheel is mostly vertical, and when the target chain crosses something that can still scroll sideways on its own — skimming a card's content must not slide the deck under it.

**The band ends cleanly, and it ends visibly.** Two rules, and the readout above depends on both. First, **the rails buy the boundary when it is within budget**: at the widths the user chose the band's far edge either lands on a slot boundary or cuts a card, and a cut has two boundaries in closed form — grow the rails until the band ends on the cut card's near edge, or shrink them until it reaches the far one — each with a price in rail width (`stripPicture`'s `growPrice` and `shrinkPrice`). The space allocator's flow key is `[cutIfUnpaid, discomfort, distance]`: a candidate total that ends the band on a boundary within `RAIL_BOUNDARY_BUDGET_PX` of the rails' preferred total has paid for it and reads zero, and every other candidate reads the cut the deck keeps when nothing is paid — one number for all of them, so that among the unpaid the comfort and distance terms decide and the rails stay where their owner put them. A boundary within budget is taken whatever the cut it removes measures, and comfort is spent to reach it before the cut is accepted, though a hard floor never is; a boundary beyond the budget is left alone, and the cut is an **honest slice** of a card, never a slice moved to clear a threshold. There is no threshold: the retired rule graded a cut clean at or past a sliver width, and its sweep manufactured exactly that width — a card clipped by a few dozen pixels that thirty pixels of rail would have uncovered. A deck whose band already ends on a boundary does not move, and the answer's rounding conserves the chosen total so a paid boundary is never missed by a pixel. Second, **the rails occlude and the margin is capped**: a flow pane's ink stops at the band edges because something opaque stands in front of it, not because anything cuts it. A rail is opaque and outranks every free card, so a card travelling toward one slides behind it and ends in the rail's own shadow — which says *there is more, behind this*, the way a physical deck says it, where a card ending at a razor says something is broken. **A card crosses the rail's gutter in plain sight before it goes behind the panel, and that crossing is the affordance** — the visible half-card is what says *there is more, behind this*, so the gutter is air the card crosses and never a leak to be covered. **A cap wide enough to reach the band edge IS the razor**, in another material: painting ground over a card at the band's edge cuts it exactly where the `clip-path` did, and the card then never reaches the rail at all, so the occlusion never once happens. This has been got wrong already — the rail-panel treatment grew the cap across the rail's whole band to have one layer for a gutter tint, and restored the cut with graph paper. A treatment that wants to paint the rail's band is a layer BELOW the cards, where the card crossing it paints over it; above them it is not ground, it is a cut. So a cap covers **only what no rail stands in**: the rail edge inset on a side where a rail is pinned — nothing at all while a panel stands flush to the window, which is where `RAIL_EDGE_INSET_PX` rests today, so that side carries no cap element whatsoever — and the band's own `IMPOSITION_GAP_PX` gap on a side where no rail stands. Each cap is full-height, above every free card and below the rail band, painting the body's ground verbatim — the canvas token and both grid gradients — anchored to the viewport by `background-attachment: fixed`, so the cap lands in phase with the page's grid rather than reading as a stripe down the window's side. The cap swallows the press it takes, so no card the user cannot see receives one, and carries the canvas-background marker, so that press still deselects exactly as a press on bare canvas always did there. Without both the far edge is a number the compositor never enforces — a straddling card paints past the band, under the rail, and out into that margin as a sliver. Together they are what makes "the half-visible card IS the affordance", asserted above for columns, true in pixels rather than only in arithmetic; and what makes the strip's bracket, which claims how much of the arrangement the band is showing, match what paints.

**The seam a card crosses on its way behind a rail is air, not a leak.** The band's own edge stands one **rail gutter** inboard of a rail's inner edge — the imposer writes a standing rail's span inset as `RAIL_EDGE_INSET_PX + width + RAIL_GUTTER_PX - GAP`, and the band's edge is that inset plus a `GAP`, so the gutter is the whole of what separates them — and a card travelling toward a rail is therefore visible in the open for `RAIL_GUTTER_PX` past the band edge before the rail takes it. The gutter grew from 5 to 12 when a pinned rail became a panel, which made that crossing MORE legible rather than less; it is the reason nothing may cover it. That is what sliding under something looks like, and it is the moment that makes the occlusion legible rather than a disappearance. Suppressing it would need a dynamic, inset-driven cap, trading away the cap's static geometry to hide the one frame worth showing. Twelve pixels against a band of several hundred is well under the sliver threshold the allocator grades against, so the strip's bracket keeps telling the truth at the resolution it claims. On a side with **no** rail the inset is zero, the cap covers the band edge exactly, and past it the card runs to the window edge — a cut every window on the machine makes, which reads as nothing at all, and which is the whole difference from a cut at an interior line.

**A rail is always divided, and its sashes are the hand's.** Every side that holds members divides its run among them by **stored shares** — one weight per member, bounded below by every member's floor — with no second arrangement to choose between and one derived exception: a rail whose floors and seams do not fit its run has nothing to divide and stands as a strip until a member leaves or the window grows. Those shares are written by a seam drag and by one verb, ***Resize Sidebars to Fit*** (⌃⌥⌘R, and the Window menu row above the six card rows), which reads each card's content element once at the moment it is invoked, stands the rail at what its cards ask for, and commits the answer down the drag's own path — after which it is the hand's division like any other, and the record outlives its members the way `order` does, so a card that leaves keeps its share and a card that arrives with none weighs 1. Nothing else moves a sash: not a resize, not a content change, not a membership change, not a relaunch — a card whose content outgrows its share scrolls inside itself, as a split pane does in every editor. See [D183], which reverses [D134] and records what went with it.

**One allocator answers for both kinds of place**: `allocatePlaceHeights` in `lib/layout-imposer.ts` takes a place's members — each a floor and a stored weight, and nothing else — with the run and the seam, and returns the standing, the heights, the strip coordinates and the strip's length. A place divides its run, with one derived exception: floors and seams that do not fit inside the run leave nothing to divide, so the place stands as a strip until a member leaves or the window grows. The pins are one expression parameterized by nothing but which two property families the place reads: seam fractions and an offset (`--tug-rail-<side>-seam-<j>`, `--tug-slot-<k>-column-offset`) or strip coordinates and an offset (`--tug-rail-<side>-strip-<j>`, `--tug-slot-<k>-strip-<j>`). A rail was the last asymmetry left between the two kinds of place: it held N members in the data model and had only ever held two on screen, so a four-member side would have divided its run into four slivers. It does not now — three modest cards divide a tall run, and six put it past the floors and onto a strip. The offset is session state on both sides, keyed by slot and by side, written by the reveal rule on activation and by a drag's one commit, and serialized by neither — the number is derivable, and a restored one would be a viewport nobody asked for onto a place that may have gained or lost members while the deck was closed.

**A sash stands between every pair of members, in both standings**, and it moves ONE boundary — a trade between the two members either side of it, since a place has a fixed run to divide and the strip's length holds. What differs by standing is the unit: a fraction of the run while the place shares it, a strip coordinate in px while it overflows — so a shared place publishes seam fractions and no strip coordinates, and an overflowing one publishes strip coordinates **and** an offset, because a strip both divides and scrolls. A weight is a claim on the run relative to one's neighbours, bounded below by every member's floor ([P04]), and the stored `shares` are left exactly as the user left them — a place that overflows and comes back divides by them again, the [L23] rule both kinds hold, and the reason re-stacking has always kept order and shares.

**The shared standing's heights are a division of the run, so a resize resolves in CSS until a floor binds.** The frames read seam fractions against a live `100%`, and a fitting place's heights are its stored shares over the run — linear in it — so a window resize re-resolves the seams exactly, in the browser's own reflow, with no JavaScript in the loop. The one term CSS cannot express is the floor bound: a member whose share would take it below its floor is held there and the deficit is redistributed to the others, which the settled-resize retune lands after the gesture. That is a deliberate trade: the approximation is confined to the resize that crosses a floor, and paying for exactness there would mean re-allocating on every resize frame.

**A column's members are keyed by pane id**, and this is the one place the two records genuinely differ. A rail keys by `componentId` because a sidebar card is a singleton — one type, one Pane, one card. A slot has no such identity to borrow: two Session cards are two cards, so `componentId` cannot name a member, and a slot holds *Panes*, each of which may itself be a tab stack, so a card id cannot either — a member named by the card it happens to be showing would be renamed by a tab switch. Pane ids are what a member actually is. The cost is persistence strength: a pane id dies with its Pane, so a closed member's entries are inert residue no reopened card will reclaim. That is accepted rather than repaired — residue names nothing standing, and the arrangement outliving its members is the same [L23] rule the rails hold. The **fallback** order, for members no stored order names, is the slot's Panes sorted by id and never their order in `state.panes`, which is z-order: taking that would make two unarranged members trade places when the user clicked the lower one, the same hazard the rails avoid by sorting into registration order.

**Column rows on the Layout card are absent, not disabled, when there is nothing to arrange** — the deliberate opposite of the rail rows. A rail row is always present because a split side dropping to one card would otherwise take the only way to un-split it away with it; a slot cannot reach that trap, since a column of one is already unsplit and has nothing to restore. Mirroring anyway would put up to six permanently dimmed rows under the two that are already there. The rows are keyed and ordered by slot, so a slot gaining a second card inserts its row in place, and the focus ladder is dense over the rows actually rendered rather than over slot numbers.

**In flow, a split column still contributes ONE extent to the strip: its widest member.** Panes sharing a slot share its place in either mode — the split divides the run, which the strip does not measure.

**On a shared place — a rail or a slot — the badge is also the gateway to the arrangement.** Its glyph states the mode — layers for a stack, rows for a split — and its menu carries the place's verbs below the member rows: *Split Vertically* in a stack; *Stack* and *Equalize Heights* in a split. The bar does not know which kind of place it is standing in and must not: it takes the mode as a fact and reports a named verb, and the Pane resolves that verb to the rail commands or the column ones from what it actually stands in. The verbs are dispatched as registered commands by the Pane, the way its width control already is, so the title bar keeps rendering from props alone. **The rows themselves differ by mode.** In a stack (slot or rail) they are a depth order, topmost first, and the check marks the one card you can see. In a split they list in **rail order, top to bottom** — the order the eye reads them in — and the check marks the **focused** member, with no row checked when focus rests outside the rail; checking the topmost there would be a claim about z-order dressed up as a claim about what you are looking at. The entry field is named `selected` for exactly that reason: it is a different Pane by arrangement, so it is named for what it means rather than for either one. **A Pane's name is one string produced in one place.** It is not any stored field: it composes the active card's registry title, the group name a multi-tab Pane carries, and the live override a card publishes on `cardTitleStore` once its identity resolves — which is why a Session card bound to a project is called `test-repo/petit-thaw` and not `Dev`. **The override replaces the registry title; it does not extend it.** A card publishes an override exactly when it has a name of its own, and that name says everything the type name said and more — `changes-rework.md` is evidently a file, and the row draws the card's document icon beside it besides — so `File : changes-rework.md` spent its first word on nothing. The registry title is the FALLBACK, what a Text card with no file open is called, not a category stamped on every name. A group name still prefixes, because it is redundant with nothing: it is the name the user gave a multi-tab Pane and no other part of the title bar carries it. That composition lives in `lib/pane-title.ts` (`composePaneTitleBarText` for the rule, `paneTitleBarTextFor` for callers holding deck state) and every surface that names a Pane goes through it: the title bar, the stack picker, and the Window menu's Pane list. A surface naming a **card** rather than a Pane takes `cardTitleTextFor` from the same module — the tab strip, which is per-tab and must not resolve the Pane's *active* card, and which carries no group name because the group prefixes the Pane once rather than once per tab. There is deliberately no card-type gate on it: the tab strip used to consult the override only for text cards, which is why a stacked Session card's tab read the literal word "Session" while its own title bar read `tugtool/stocky-pixie`. It is written down because the alternative already happened — a second, simpler rule over `CardState.title` lived in the menu-state projection, and it named a bound Session card `Untitled` in every list while its title bar named it properly. A surface that resolves the name itself will drift from the title bar the moment a card's identity becomes dynamic.

The stack is **derived**, never stored (`slotStackOf` over `DeckState.panes`), and the picker is built from that store data rather than from revealed DOM, so it needs nothing from the occlusion controller and works the same whether the Panes behind are hidden, peeking, or offscreen. Cmd-**drag** still drags, because the Cmd decision is resolved at the gesture's ending — in the drag machine's own no-travel branch — and a drag that travels never reaches it. See [D123].

#### Bullseye is a presentation over a mode, not a fourth mode

The three modes above stay mutually exclusive. **Bullseye** ([D131]) is not a fourth one: it is a temporary presentation that **supersedes** whichever mode a Pane holds, for as long as that Pane holds the first responder. Reached by ⌃⌘B or Window ▸ Bullseye, it paints the frame as a **one-up placement at comfy** — `imposeStyle({ slot: 0, count: 1 }, comfyWidth, pinnedFrame)`, which is the imposer's existing definition of centred-in-the-band rather than new centring math — over the full vertical run, with every other Pane receded. **A rail holds it like any other Pane** — its width, its side, and therefore the band's inset are all untouched while it does, so the place it stands in is *reserved*, not reclaimed, and it drops back onto its edge on exit. Precedence over the pinned branch is what makes that work rather than a defence.

**It writes no geometry, so leaving it restores nothing.** Entering, holding, and leaving never call `movePane`, `centerPane`, or `setPaneWidth`: the only state change is `bullseyePaneId`, which is session-only and absent from `serialize()`, so a reload always comes back un-bullseyed. A bullseyed Pane's stored `position` / `size` / `slot` / `widthPreset` are last-known values in exactly the sense a pinned or imposed Pane's already are — and the width popup and Window ▸ Slim/Comfy/Wide keep showing the width the **store** holds, which is the width the frame returns to. Not writing four fields is strictly stronger than restoring them correctly.

**Four doors leave it, in two shapes.** Any path that writes a Pane's `position`, `size`, or `slot` releases *that* Pane — one helper, `_clearBullseyeFor`, honored by `movePane`, `setContentWidth`, and `assignCardToSlot`; the rule is stated over the mutation rather than over the caller, which is the same rule that already evicts a derived Pane on a manual gesture. And any move of the first responder out of the Pane ends it, cleared once from `_flipFirstResponder`, the single entry point for first-responder transitions — so the click, the ⌘R picker, both rings, the sidebar chords, and the canvas-background deselect are all covered, along with every focus path added later. A tab switch *inside* the bullseyed Pane keeps the posture: the question is whether the Pane hosts the responder, not which of its cards does. `bullseyePaneIdOf` derives on top of both, so a stale id is unreadable in the window before a flip commits.

**Every other content Pane leaves the canvas while bullseye holds**, sliding off the canvas and back on exit — a Pane that is merely dimmed is still one you can read. The displacement is one `left` override at render, so it writes nothing and returns to the pixel. **Which side it leaves by is read against the bullseyed card, not against the canvas**: Panes left of it go left, Panes right of it go right, so no Pane ever crosses the card arriving at the middle and relative order holds throughout the move. Both centres come from RESOLVED pins, never from stored `position.x`, which for a derived Pane is the superseded last-known value and would send every card in an imposition out the same side; the comparison is a CSS clamp rather than a measurement. It rides the existing FLIP settle out and back — **both clamp bounds park the Pane just past the canvas edge rather than a whole canvas beyond it**, because the settle's spring spends most of its distance early and a Pane sent that far is gone before the eye can follow it. The motion is what says "moved aside" rather than "closed". **Rails are excluded**: a rail that left would take the band's insets with it and the bullseyed card would jump the moment the posture began, so rails keep their pins and recede in place.

**The recede is the existing inactive-content dim at much deeper values**, on the two `.tug-pane-chrome` pseudo layers under a `data-bullseye` hook — never `.tug-pane-scrim`, which is the pane-modal layer and blocks pointer events. Deep is load-bearing: the inactive dim it builds on moves the card surface roughly 4%, so a small multiple of it reads as no change at all. Bullseye must stay non-modal, because clicking another Pane is one of its exit doors. Nothing may add `filter`, `transform`, `backdrop-filter`, `contain`, or `will-change` to `.tug-pane` for it: each makes every receded frame a containing block for its `position: fixed` descendants, and `TugSheet` portals into the frame while completion popups and banners position from viewport coordinates.

**Three doors reach it, over two actions.** The title bar's **target button** — a `lucide` `Target`, immediately behind the stack badge on every Pane, rails included — dispatches the pane-addressed **`set-bullseye`** taking `{ paneId }`: the button you pressed is the Pane you meant. ⌃⌘B and Window ▸ Bullseye stay selection-relative, and the canvas hands off to `set-bullseye` once it has resolved which Pane the selection is in, so all three land on one store call. This is the shape `set-pane-width` → `set-card-width` already has, and the reason it is now two actions rather than one: a button can name a Pane the selection is not in, and a chord cannot.

The button reports the posture as well as setting it — `data-on` and `aria-pressed` from the DERIVED id, never from a local toggle — because a control that put the deck in a posture has to say so for the second press to read as the exit it is. A bullseyed rail also drops its resize handle for the duration: that handle drags the *rail's* width, measured from the deck edge it is pinned to, and the frame is not standing there.

Both derived modes resolve at render, in CSS, from custom properties (`--tug-imposer-inset-left` / `--tug-imposer-inset-right` for the span) rather than from a measured-and-committed rect. The deck installs no resize observation of any kind: the browser reflows derived Panes on a window resize or a rail drag for free ([L06]). A derived Pane's stored `position`/`size` therefore hold last-known values, refreshed when the Pane leaves the mode; anything needing the truth measures the frame.

**Every horizontal pin is emitted as `left`**, measured from the left inset, in one shape on every deck. A frame positioned by one property in one form can *transition* between two arrangements; one that switched to `right`, or to a bare length where the other side had a percentage, could only cut. That is what lets a change to the `imposition` record settle rather than teleport: `DeckCanvas` marks the frames' container with `data-imposer-settling` for `IMPOSITION_SETTLE_MS` and every derived frame crosses to its new place.

**A pinned rail crosses by a number, not by a pin.** Its two anchors are a bare length on the left and a percentage on the right, which are not the same kind of value and so have nothing to interpolate — and a `0%` added to the left one is simplified straight back out at computed-value time. So the side is carried as a registered `<number>` custom property, `--tugx-rail-side` (0 left, 1 right), and the pin is one expression that mixes the anchors by it. Animating the number re-resolves the pin on every frame. Two consequences worth holding on to: `left` must be **absent** from the rail frame's transition list, or the two drivers fight over one property; and the side property's own transition is declared **ungated**, because WebKit will not start a transition on a registered custom property whose covering `transition-property` arrives in the same style change as the value — and nothing but a re-imposition ever writes the side, so there is nothing to gate it against.

**The settle is armed from the store, not from a render.** WebKit will not start a transition on a property whose covering `transition-property` arrives in the same style change as the value — so arming `data-imposer-settling` in a layout effect, which runs after React has already written the new `left`, puts both in one change and the frame cuts. `DeckCanvas` subscribes to the deck store directly: the subscriber runs before the re-render it causes, sets the attribute, and flushes it as a style change of its own (reading the resolved duration back), so the geometry arrives into a transition already in force. This is not a nicety — before it, a slot chosen with the pointer animated and the same slot chosen with the keyboard cut, because a pointer-down happens to activate a pane and armed the settle one style change early by accident.

**Motion curves are functions, not Bézier literals.** `lib/unit-functions.ts` holds the catalogue — a port of UpKit's `UPUnitFunction`, plus a critically damped spring — and `cssEasing` samples any of them into a CSS `linear()`. The settle names its curve once, as `IMPOSITION_SETTLE_EASING`; `DeckCanvas` writes it and the duration onto the frames' container as `--tugx-imposer-settle-easing` / `--tugx-imposer-settle-duration`, so a curve is swapped at its definition and tuned live by overriding the property.

**A gesture commits only once the pointer has travelled.** Under `DRAG_MOVE_THRESHOLD_PX` a title-bar press is a click: it focuses the Pane and commits nothing, and a derived Pane keeps the slot or the pin it started with. The conversion to free pixel geometry happens at the threshold crossing, not at pointer-down — so releasing a rail card from its pin takes an actual drag, which is the only gesture that means it.

#### Chrome has three heights, and each one says what kind of thing the card is

A Pane's title bar wears one of three tiers, and which one is not a style choice — it is the card saying what it is.

| Tier | Height | Ground | Who wears it |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Utility** | 36px | tinted title band | the default: Settings, Keyboard, About, the gallery cards |
| **Document** | 72px masthead | tinted title band | a card that IS a document and says its own name: Text, File viewer, Diff — and the Session card, whose masthead is its own ([D132]) |
| **Rail** | 32px | flush, on the pane's own surface | a tool pinned to a deck edge: Cards, Jots, Layout |

A **document masthead** carries three lines. The Session card's are `project/callsign` with the Pane controls, the session's description, the beat line ([D186]), and a trailing telemetry popover; every other document card's are a name, where the document lives, and one quieter fact about it — the Text card's save state, the File viewer's kind and page count, the Diff's file and line counts. The two mount the same `TugSessionRow` on the same `masthead-frame.css` tier, which is what keeps them the same object rather than two ladders that resemble each other. Each line above the last reserves the control cluster back for itself, because the frame deliberately pulls out from under it so the third line can run the full width.

**The control cluster has an order, and the stack badge leads it.** The badge is the one control there that is about the Pane's *place* rather than about the Pane, so the row reads left to right as "one of two — and here is what you can do to it"; it is also the only position that holds still, since every control behind it can be absent on a given card. A card that contributes a control of its own — today only the Session masthead's telemetry widget — **mounts it inside that cluster**, portaled into a host the Pane renders directly after the badge. It is a real member of the row, not a box positioned beside it. That distinction is the whole of the fix: the widget used to be absolutely positioned against the cluster's measured width, which computed correctly and landed one place ahead of the badge, so on the single card carrying both, the control that says *where you are* came second. Membership pays twice — the Pane's own measurement of the cluster now covers the widget, so the masthead's title line no longer reserves a hand-typed 28px box beside a measured one.

**The second line answers *where*, and it is not a second printing of the first.** A path truncates in the **middle** — `TugPath`, the same mechanism the transcript's tool-block headers wear — because a path's two informative ends are its root and its filename, and end truncation destroys the one the reader was looking for. A card whose path source is repo-relative resolves it against its root before publishing: a file at a repo's root would otherwise put the same filename on both lines and spend one of them saying nothing.

A **rail** is not a card in the slot band, and the layout imposer has always known it. Its chrome says so too: a flush ground instead of the tinted band, an uppercase tracked label, a tinted glyph, and three hairline racing stripes. Rail-ness comes from the card's registered **`layoutRole: "sidebar"`** — what the card *is* — and never from `sidebarSide`, which says only where a rail currently stands. A released rail card is still a tool; its livery does not blink when it leaves its pin. Everything the rail draws paints from the `--tug7-element-global-*` family, never the card-titlebar family, because a rail has given up the tinted band and its ground is the pane's own content surface.

**A pinned rail is a panel of the window, not a card on the paper.** Under the shipped treatment (`data-rail-treatment="panel"`, stamped once on the deck root from `RAIL_TREATMENT`) a rail standing at its edge is flush to the window's top and outer edge with square corners, the sunken surface, and a strong hairline on its inner edge alone, and it casts a one-sided shadow from that inner edge across most of the gutter — the rail stands above every content card, and the shadow is that z-order made visible, the depth between a panel and the paper rather than air between two cards. **The shadow is the canvas's, one strip per side, never one per member**: a strip drawn by each member pane is only as continuous as the member frames are and broke at every seam, which contradicts the panel's own reading at exactly the boundary the panel is about — a rail is ONE flush surface from window top to foot, so the thing that says it stands above the cards has no seam in it either. It spans the rail's run rather than the window, so it stops at the rail's foot instead of running on beside the maker strip; its foot is flush in a release build and meets the maker strip with no air in a maker one. Two named lengths carry its geometry where the card gap used to by coincidence of value: the **rail edge inset** (`RAIL_EDGE_INSET_PX`, `--tug-rail-edge-inset`, 0) is the rail's stand-off from the window on its outer side and top, and the **rail gutter** (`RAIL_GUTTER_PX`, `--tug-rail-gutter`, 12) is the air between the rail's inner edge and the band the cards stand in. `IMPOSITION_GAP_PX` stays the seam between cards and between a split column's members, so the deck's rhythm does not move with the rail; a split rail's members keep the **rail seam** (`RAIL_SEAM_PX`, 0) instead — they touch and meet at one hairline, because a gap between them is the content cards' rhythm and not the panel's. **A rail's hairlines mark boundaries, and the window's own edges get none** ([D181] as amended): a seated member rules only its inner edge against the gutter and, when a member stands below it, its bottom edge as the seam's one line — no top rules, no bottom rule on the last member (`data-rail-member-last`), so a rail of one is one flush panel from window top to window bottom and an overflowing rail's clipped members have nothing to hide. A rail card in the air regains the whole card frame for the length of `data-gesture`, lift to landing, on the same reasoning as the released rail below — and while any card is in the air (the canvas's `data-carrying`) the SEATED members fill in every rule too, because a carry makes new boundaries: the place a lifted member left is a gap its neighbours now border, and every rail place is somewhere the drop could land. The drop outline itself is square, because it marks a place and a place has the arrangement's corners, not a card's. The margin cap paints that whole margin, edge to gutter, one layer below the rails, and the treatment's tint is declared at strength zero, so the tinted and ruled alternatives are one CSS block away and never a second frame. A released rail keeps its livery and regains a card's frame, because a thing afloat over cards has to read as droppable. The choice is [D181].

**The height is fixed and ratified, never content-driven.** The masthead never reflows; overflow truncates. Chrome that changed height as its text changed would move every card in the Pane while the user was reading.

**The Pane owns the slot; the card's own family owns what is inside it.** `TugPane` opens the taller tier and sizes it, then mounts a tugways `SessionMasthead` into it, keyed by session id. That split is the point: chrome stays the Pane's ([L09]) without the digest feeder, the dwell queue, the compaction pin, the sparkline series and the telemetry popover moving into chrome code. The precedent is the Session card already rendering `TugPaneBanner` — Pane-class furniture, card-class content inside it.

**Which card the Pane asks is the ACTIVE one** — for the masthead and for the rail role alike. A card publishes a masthead sidecar beside its `cardTitleStore` string, and the Pane wears the masthead exactly when its frontmost tab publishes one — so the chrome swaps 36↔72 on tab switch and the content region reflows with it. A pinned 72px chrome over a Text tab would caption one card with another card's identity. A stacked Session card contributes nothing to the chrome; its identity still reads on its tab and in the stack picker.

**A card publishes its masthead from MOUNT, not on data arrival.** The tier's height is fixed so chrome does not move under a reader, and a card that grew from 36px to 72px the moment its diff resolved would be exactly that failure. So a document card publishes the payload as soon as it is a document at all, with `description` and `detail` filling in later. A tab switch may change the tier; loading may not.

**And what it publishes for a line it cannot fill is a STAND-IN, never `null`.** The tier is a fixed three lines, so an absent description is not a shorter masthead — it is a hole between two filled lines, with the quieter fact stranded below it. This is the session masthead's description ladder ([D132]) applied to documents: a Text card that is not a file yet says *No file*, a Project Diff still reading says *Reading the diff…*, one with no repository behind it says *No repository here*. A stand-in is published as such (`descriptionStandIn`), and on a document tier it is set in **italic** as well as a step quieter — the line's real content is a path, so "where it is" and "there is no where yet" differ as type and not only as a shade of gray. The stand-in answers the line's own question rather than restating the state line below it: the second line says where, the third says how it stands. Its third line follows the same discipline: a buffer nothing has ever written says **Draft**, because "Saved" is a claim about an act, and clean is not the same fact for a draft as it is for a file.

**The height travels as a Pane-scoped custom property, `--tugx-pane-chrome-height`** — `--tug-masthead-height` when the masthead is up, `--tug-rail-chrome-height` on a rail, `--tug-chrome-height` otherwise — and only the sites that are really measuring *the title-bar tier* follow it: the bar itself, the Pane scrim's top, the sheet clip's top, and the Pane banner's top. The tab bar's own metrics stay on `--tug-chrome-height`, because **the masthead and the tab bar stack rather than merge**: a multi-tab Session Pane's chrome is 72 + 36, masthead on top, an ordinary 36px tab row beneath it. Re-pointing a tab metric at the Pane property would give a masthead Pane 72px-tall tabs. The sheet needs only its clip's top re-pointed; the panel's in-pane height falls out of the measured clip rect for free, and the standalone `max-height` fallback must stay on the global token because a Pane-scoped property is undefined where there is no Pane.

**Both tiers stamp their role on the PANE, not only on the bar** — `data-masthead="true"` and `data-role="sidebar"` — for the same reason: the height has to be declared where the scrim, the sheet clip, and the banner can see it. CSS cannot derive it from the bar's own attribute, because `:has()` does not invalidate on a descendant attribute change in WebKit. The rail's ink, stripes, and label stay keyed on the bar's attribute, since those style the bar. `data-rail-side` is a different bit and is not redundant with `data-role`: it carries which edge a rail is pinned to, and a released rail has rail chrome with no side.

The swap is safe against [L26] for a structural reason worth stating rather than relying on: `CardTitleBar` and `.tug-pane-body` are **siblings**, and `.tug-pane-content` is an empty `ref`'d div that cards portal into. A chrome-mode branch therefore has no card subtree to re-key or reparent, so the tab switch cannot cost the content region its mount identity. Keep that discipline — the chrome slot stays a sibling, nothing in the content region's key derives from chrome mode, and the height travels as a custom property ([L06]) — and keep the scroll-survival assertion that pins it.

### Card

The content identity. A Card has a stable id, a `componentId` that names its content type, a `title`, a `closable` flag, and an optional `state` bag carrying per-content persistence (scroll, selection, content-specific payload). Cards are the durable identity that survives cross-Pane moves — detach, merge, reorder all preserve a Card's id and its React-tree identity (because `CardHost` portals into the host Pane's DOM; it is never remounted).

| Owner | Responsibility |
|-------|---------------|
| `CardHost` (component) | Wraps the registered content factory with the four per-content contexts (`CardDataProvider`, `CardPropertyContext`, `CardPersistenceContext`, `CardDirtyContext`) + the responder scope keyed by `cardId` |
| `CardState` (type) | `{ id, componentId, title, closable, state? }` |
| `CardStateBag` (type) | `{ scroll?, selection?, content? }` — the per-content persistence payload |
| Card content component | The body registered via `registerCard(componentId, { contentFactory, defaultMeta, ... })` |

**A Card is not a responder for Pane state.** Actions like `close` that target the Pane walk up past the Card's own responder scope and are handled by the Pane or by DeckManager. A Card *is* a responder for content actions its body implements (`cut`, `copy`, `paste`, `selectAll`, `undo`, `redo`, and any custom actions).

## Tabs Are Not a Data Concept

**A tab is a UI affordance that appears on a Pane when that Pane's `cardIds.length > 1`.** `TugTabBar` is presentational: it renders one tab per `cardId`, dispatches `selectTab` and `closeTab` actions, and disappears when the Pane has only one card. There is no "Tab" type, no `TabState`, no `tabId`. The identity the tab strip surfaces is the underlying Card's `id`.

This is why the vocabulary sweep that produced this document removed every `tabId` parameter name in favor of `cardId`: there is no tab identity separate from a card identity.

| Pane's card count | Tab strip |
|---|---|
| 1 | Hidden |
| 2+ | Rendered; one tab per `cardId`; the Pane's `activeCardId` shows as selected |

## Naming Rules

Each layer of the system uses a distinct prefix so a reader can identify what a name refers to from its prefix alone.

### Components

| Prefix | Meaning | Examples |
|--------|---------|----------|
| `TugPane*` | Components that render Pane-level chrome | `TugPane`, `TugPaneBanner`, `TugTabBar` |

The `Tug` prefix marks components (things that render JSX). Card-model *types and hooks* drop the prefix — they describe content identity, not chrome.

## Wire Contract

The Deck → Pane → Card vocabulary flows through every serialization surface without translation.

### v4 layout blob (tugbank `dev.tugapp.deck.layout`)

```jsonc
{
  "version": 4,
  "cards": [
    { "id": "card-abc", "componentId": "hello", "title": "Hello", "closable": true }
  ],
  "panes": [
    {
      "id": "pane-xyz",
      "position": { "x": 100, "y": 100 },
      "size":     { "width": 400, "height": 300 },
      "cardIds":       ["card-abc"],
      "activeCardId":  "card-abc",
      "title":         "",
      "acceptsFamilies": ["standard"],
      "slot": 1
    }
  ],
  "activePaneId": "pane-xyz",
  "imposition": "three-up"
}
```

`imposition` is a `{ kind?, contentWidth?, layout?, sidebars, rails? }` record on the deck; `slot` and `widthPreset` are additive-optional on the Pane. `layout` (`"fit"` | `"flow"`) and `rails` are additive-optional in the same spirit and read the same defensive way: absent means flow and stacked respectively — flow because that is the arrangement a deck opens in when it has never chosen, and an unreadable value is dropped rather than defaulted, because a geometry mode and a rail arrangement are things the user chose. The flow **offset** is deliberately not here: it is session state, derivable by re-activating any card. An absent `kind` means "no imposition / Pane not imposed", which is exactly the pre-imposer semantics. The value has widened without a version bump — from a bare kind string to the record — because both shapes parse: an absent `contentWidth` reads as comfy (which is the width every content card shipped at, so the migration is behavior-preserving), and a blob older than the `sidebars` map carries placements this parser does not read, whose ids are unregistered now and are swept by `filterDeckStateByRegistration` on the same load. Retired fields are dropped on read rather than honored: a `collapsed` Pane comes back expanded, and a blob from the build whose rails divided vertically loses its `sidebarSplit` and its per-card `order` — reading those back would reinstate a geometry this build cannot paint. A sidebar entry is therefore rebuilt field by field rather than spread, so a blob cannot smuggle a field the code no longer knows about into the record. The rest of the read path is defensive in the same spirit: an unrecognized kind drops the arrangement and every `slot` with it; a `slot` needs a valid kind, a non-negative integer, and a Pane hosting no sidebar card; an out-of-range slot clamps to the kind's last slot. A Pane with a surviving `slot`, and a sidebar Pane, both skip the canvas-fit clamp — their geometry derives at render.

Pre-v4 blobs used `windows` and `activeWindowId` and a different embedded-card shape. `serialization.ts` migrates on read; writes are always v4. The `focusedCardId` pointer for reload focus restoration is stored in a separate tugbank domain (`dev.tugapp.deck.focused`), not inside the layout blob.

### Per-card state (tugbank `dev.tugapp.deck.cardstate/{cardId}`)

One row per Card. The row key is the Card's id. The value is the `CardStateBag` — scroll position, saved selection, content payload.

### IPC actions (Swift ↔ deck)

| Action | Payload | Source | Purpose |
|--------|---------|--------|---------|
| `focus-pane` | `{ paneId }` | Swift menu → web | Activate a Pane by id; promotes its `activeCardId` as first responder |
| `add-card-to-active-pane` | none | Swift menu → web | Add a new card to the currently-active Pane |
| `close` | none | Swift menu → web | Dispatch through the responder chain; resolved by Pane or DeckManager (menu label "Close Card" or "Close Pane" depending on card count) |

### Swift menu vocabulary

`AppDelegate.swift` names menu items to match this model:

- **File ▸ Close Card / Close Pane** — dynamic label. When the active Pane holds more than one card, the label is "Close Card" (closes the active card only). When it holds exactly one, the label is "Close Pane" (closes the last card, removing the Pane).
- **Dev ▸ Add Card to Active Pane** — explicit Pane-scoped action.

A developer changing Swift menu strings must preserve this vocabulary: Card-level gestures say "Card"; Pane-level gestures say "Pane".

## The deck is placed, never scrolled

**Every Pane's title bar is on the Deck, and the Deck itself never moves.** A title bar above the Deck's top edge cannot be grabbed, and since nothing in the Deck scrolls there is no gesture that brings it back: the Pane is stranded and the user is stuck with a Card they cannot address. The rule is therefore enforced, not merely intended, and at three independent layers — because the incident that produced it (2026-07-28: one ⌥⌘↑ parked every card's title bar above the window top) came from a layer nobody was watching.

| Layer | The law | Where it lives |
|---|---|---|
| Geometry | No Pane commits with `position.y` above the Deck top | `DeckState` invariant 7 + `clampPanesToDeck`, applied in `DeckManager.notify` — the one commit point every mutation passes through, so restore-from-disk and detach are covered along with drag |
| Structure | The page is not a scroller | `overflow: clip` on the Deck root (`deck-canvas.tsx`) |
| Behavior | A scroller scrolls itself and no ancestor | `SmartScroll.scrollToElement` computes its own delta; `revealFocusTarget` walks scrollports minimally |

The structural layer is the subtle one and worth stating plainly. **The canvas does not scroll — but the page underneath it can.** A Pane parked so its frame reaches past a window edge overflows the Deck root; because `#deck-container` is unpositioned, that overflow resolves against the viewport and lands in `<body>`'s scroll box, which `globals.css` renders invisible (`overflow: hidden`) but leaves perfectly scrollable from script. Anything that walks ancestor scrollports — a stray `scrollIntoView`, a browser focus reveal — can then scroll the *page*, which slides the whole Deck under the window with no scrollbar, no wheel target, and nothing to put it back. `clip` rather than `hidden` is what closes it: it clips at the same box but forms no scroll container, so the range never exists to be spent.

Imposed Panes get the rule twice over, as they should — Tug places them itself. Their frame is derived, pinned a gap below the canvas top in CSS (`imposeStyle`), so the geometry law holds for them by construction rather than by clamp; the structural and behavioral layers protect them exactly as they protect free Panes. Pinned by `at0283-page-not-a-scroller` and `at0284-title-bar-floor`.

## A card's place is said in three vocabularies

**A card on the imposed deck stands at three coordinates, and each has one badge and one vocabulary that never crosses into another's.** The slot it holds is a **number in a box**. The stack it shares is a **count over three slices**. The band it occupies in a split is a **letter over three rungs** — A the topmost. Both glyphs mark the element the card sits at. All three draw in the same footprint, so a slot chip and a column badge beside it read left to right as one address: `1A`, `2C`.

The number/letter split is not decoration, and it is the part worth stating, because the obvious symmetry — a letter for both — is wrong. **The character says the fact the eye cannot get.** A split hides nothing, since every band is on screen at once, so there the character's job is *naming*: the letter is an address, matched against the same letter on the card's Cards row. A stack hides everything behind the front card, and the quantity behind is exactly what no amount of looking will give you — so there the character is a count. Letters therefore mean *split* wherever they appear, on every surface.

**Whether a place *draws* split is `columnDrawsSplit`, and that is a question only the geometry asks.** `mode` says what the arrangement IS; the drawing says what a run is actually divided into, and those come apart at exactly one card: membership churn never destroys an arrangement, so a slot that was split and lost a member keeps `mode: "split"` waiting for it to return while rendering its one member across the whole undivided run. Every reader that allocates a run, pins a frame, places a seam, or walks bands reads that one function, because the picture cannot afford two opinions about it.

**A badge names the arrangement the place is SET to, exactly as a mark does.** `columnBadgeFactsOf` asks `columnModeOf` and nothing else, so a slot split while it holds one card wears `A` from the moment the verb commits, on its masthead and on its Cards row alike. The badge was gated on a second member once, on the argument that a lone card is in no band — and that made the only surface able to report the act report it as having failed: the user pressed Split, the run legitimately did not divide, the badge went on reading `1`, and the letter turned up later when a neighbour arrived, as a surprise attributed to the wrong gesture. An arrangement standing one member deep is a real state the user asked for and the badge is what says so; the undivided run beside it is the geometry telling the truth about the same slot from the other side. Both surfaces read the one selector, so they cannot drift apart again in either direction.

**The Layout card states a place's arrangement on the picture of the deck, not in a row beside it.** Its rows are the questions the picture cannot ask: the deck-wide three — Slots, Imposition, Width — and one boot-fixed row per registered sidebar card reading `Off · Left · Right`, because whether a card is on the deck at all is exactly what a drawing of the deck does not draw, and a hidden card's row is the one door that shows it (its side segments show it on the side they name; `set-sidebar-open` is the plain placement verb, deliberately not the focus-moving three-state toggle the View menu runs). The arrangement questions — the ones about what IS on screen — are asked on the miniature itself: `LayoutPlaces` marks each occupied slot with the stack/split glyph, and that is the schematic's whole vocabulary — the rails are drawn and not marked, since a rail is always divided and has no second arrangement to state. Placement is deliberately NOT a mark: which side a card holds is the same axis as whether it is there at all, the rows ask that one question with all three answers, and a second door on the picture would be a duplicate that cannot say Off. The sides drawn and marked are counted from the OPEN cards, not the registered ones, because the picture draws what stands and a closed card drawn standing on a rail is a resting lie. The section used to ask all of it in rows, up to thirteen under a drawing that had just drawn every one of them, so the reader's eye joined "Column 3" to the third block on every read and the panel's height moved as cards did. The overlay costs none of that and scales sideways for nothing. It replicates the drawing's own flex box and takes its numbers from `miniatureGeometry`, the arithmetic the drawing consumes, because a second opinion about where a block is would be off by the frame's padding and its gaps at every size; and it stands OUTSIDE the plan's layers, which swap by `display`, since a mark parked inside the committed one would vanish the moment its own hover raised a preview and then oscillate. The drawing stays a readout and takes no pointer; the marks are what answer a hand — and each mark's words state what the place is set to before what the press would do, because the consequence half is inferable from the glyph and the present half is not.

**A mark reads the arrangement a place is SET to, and every occupied place has one.** The badge's rule above, on the Layout card's surface — this is where it was first written down, and the badges are now held to it too. The column rows were gated on a slot holding two or more cards, on the argument that a column of one is already unsplit and has nothing to restore — which the preceding paragraph shows is false: the slot goes on storing `split`, drawn honestly as one undivided card, and with no row there was no way to say otherwise. It sat there until a second card arrived and resurfaced as a surprise. So a mark is given to every slot the drawing draws, occupied or not, drawn from `columnModeOf` rather than from what is on screen — the rails carry none, since a rail is always divided and a mark saying so every time would be a glyph with nothing to distinguish, and a place standing one card deep, or none, wears it at **the same weight as every other** and stays pressable. An arrangement outlives its membership all the way to zero, and a drawn block with no mark reads as a hole in the instrument rather than as a fact about the deck. The membership itself is not drawn: a first cut whispered the places with fewer than two cards under them, and two tints in one picture read as a rendering fault before they read as a distinction — one about how many cards stand somewhere, which is not the question a mark answers. The rails had always refused this gate for their own rows; the columns are now held to the same rule, on a surface cheap enough to afford it.

**A mark carries the arrangement NOT in force, which is what lets one control serve a two-valued fact.** Pressing it sets the other thing, so the same element goes each way and there is no segmented pair to keep in sync with the glyph. The overlay is **one** stop in the walk with a cursor over its marks — the shape every segmented row already has, and the reason Tab does not crawl the drawing — and Space there is the press. Every commit leaves by `selectValue` on the sender ids the rows used, so the section's one responder still does the dispatching and the funnel is unchanged ([L11], [L30]); the overlay never reaches a command itself.

**A mark does not audition, and the rows do.** The distinction is not squeamishness about motion: a row's answers are words whose effect on the deck is genuinely hard to picture (`Comfy`, `Flow`, `Three Up`), and swapping the whole plan is the cheapest way to show what one would do. A mark's answer is a two-state toggle whose effect **is the glyph it wears**, so an audition shows the reader a picture they can already read off the control, and it costs something real: marks stand a few pixels apart, so a pointer crossing from one to the next raised a layer, dropped to committed for the width of the gap, and raised the next — the section strobing as the hand moved. So a mark answers a hand the way any button does, with its own hover surface and the accent on its glyph, and the keyboard cursor wears that same appearance because standing on a mark and pointing at one mean the same thing here. No preview layer is generated for an arrangement, either: a layer nothing can raise is dead weight in the DOM, not a spare. **And the pointer auditions nothing at all — the rows preview under the keyboard cursor only.** A hover-intent clock came first, a beat before raising and a hold before dropping, and it did stop the strobe. What it could not touch is the thing beneath the strobe: a hand travelling to the control it means to press passes over three or four others on the way, and a drawing that answers each of them restates the section's largest, most detailed element while the reader is doing nothing but moving their hand. A clock only decides how gracefully that happens. So a pointer states nothing and presses instead, which is the rule the drawing itself already obeys. The keyboard keeps its audition because a movement cursor is not travel: it is always ON a mark, it arrives one deliberate key at a time, and comparing arrangements before Space makes one real is exactly what the arrows are for ([P24]). One caution worth carrying: the focus engine excludes any registered element computing `pointer-events: none` (`isRecordInteractive`), so an overlay made inert to let hovers cross it would be registered, visible, and silently unreachable by keyboard.

**A preview carries the marks with it.** Every plan layer renders the places overlay a second time, as a **ghost**: inert spans restating every mark at THAT layer's geometry. The live overlay's marks drop to opacity zero while any preview shows — opacity rather than `display`, so the overlay keeps its box and the drawing beneath it does not reflow — so exactly one legend stands on the drawing at a time and it is the honest one. Without this, a preview moved the deck under marks that stayed at the committed positions — the picture auditioning a change while its own legend contradicted it. The ghost takes no pointer at all, root and descendants both; being inside the `aria-hidden` plan, it is spans rather than buttons, so it hides nothing from assistive technology that was not already stated in words by the live overlay.

**The glyph says where in the run the card sits, and it says it for both kinds.** A stack marked its front slice unconditionally once, on the argument that a badge stands on the card you can see. That holds for a badge on a pane's own title bar and nowhere else: a Cards row names a card that may be buried three deep, and marking the front slice there said *this one is on top* about a card that is not. The pane cluster does not contradict it, because it draws no mark at all. The index is read through `columnMoveOrder`, so the marked end and the direction *Move Up in Column* travels cannot disagree — and for a stack that means z-order, front first, since a stack's members all draw the same rect and z is the only ordering a reader can perceive.

Two consequences follow, and both are intended:

- **The glyph names a region; the character names the position.** Three rungs say top, middle, or bottom however deep the split runs, so every interior band of a column of five lights the same middle rung while its letter stays exact. Drawing one rung per member was tried and does not survive the badge's size.
- **Nothing subdivides the box.** The slot badge works because it is one character in one rectangle; every design that drew the arrangement *inside* the badge died at that size. So the glyph goes *behind* the character as quiet scenery, each of its elements painted as the chip beside it, and the character knocked clear of the strokes by a shadow in whatever it stands on.

**The badge wears whatever the slot chip beside it is wearing.** That is the whole colour rule, and it is a rule about the neighbour rather than a palette, because the badge is drawn in the chip's footprint so the two read as one address — and an address written in two hands is two facts. So the chip's colours are the badge's colours, its line is the chip's 1px border, and where the chip has no state to show the badge shows none either. Everything derives from `currentColor` unless a mount says otherwise, which is what makes the two shipped looks fall out of one rule instead of out of two stylesheets kept in step:

- **In the pane's title bar** the badge is handed `currentColor` and simply *is* the cluster's ink, tracking rest, hover, pressed, menu-open and pane focus with the ghost buttons beside it, without a state rule of its own. The chip there is grey chrome with no selection state at rest, so the badge is monochrome.
- **On a Cards row** every element of the glyph is painted as a chip: an unlit one takes the resting chip's fill, border and number ink, and the element the card occupies takes the selected chip's fill and border. The chip there *is* a control with a selection state, so the badge has one too. The tokens are named, not approximated — a colour picked to look close is a second opinion about the run, and it drifts the first time the run is retuned.

What the badge never does is spend an accent on its own initiative. It carried one once — the lit element in the action colour on every surface, including a title bar where nothing was selecting anything — which said *live* about the most inert fact the deck has, since where a card is sitting is true whether or not anybody is reaching for it. That is the argument `CardSlotBadge` settled for the chip; the accent now appears exactly where the run beside it is already spending one.

**Whether the glyph marks the level at all is the surface's call**, through `showLevel`. The character always says it; the mark is a second telling, and it earns its keep only where something nearby teaches the reading — a Cards row's slot picker, whose selection fill it borrows. In a title bar there is nothing to teach it and no room to say it: a lit rung there is a fraction of a pixel of extra weight in a ladder the height of a lowercase letter, which the eye takes as grit on the badge rather than as an answer, competing with a character that is stating the same thing outright. So the cluster's badge draws the run and stops. The root's `data-lit` is unaffected either way — that is the computed region, a fact about the card, true whether or not the glyph is drawing it.

`TugColumnBadge` (`components/tugways/tug-column-badge.tsx`) is the one drawing of the second and third coordinates; `CardSlotBadge` is the first. A rail and a column are the same kind of place, so a split rail's members wear the same letters a split column's do. Pinned by `at0401`, `at0455`, and `at0467`.

**A place one card deep is still a place, and the badge says so — on every surface that draws one.** It reads `1`, which is quiet but true, and the reason it draws at all is that the badge is the only door to the arrange verbs: gating it on already having a neighbour left the one card that most needs *Split Vertically* — a card alone in its column — with no way to reach it and no way to learn that splitting was a thing columns did. The condition is holding a place, not depth. The single case that still draws nothing is a pane standing in no place at all, where a chip would claim a position the pane does not hold.

The rule is stated for every surface because it was once true of only one. The pane's cluster went unconditional and the Cards row did not, so the same card's masthead said it stood somewhere while its Cards row said it stood nowhere — and the depth test lived in `columnBadgeFactsOf`, which is the deck's *one* reading of its columns and therefore the last place a per-surface opinion belongs. A surface may re-pair the badge's ink; it does not get its own answer about whether the card has a place. That the Cards card's Sessions cell and its ordinary card row are two different components is an implementation fact, not a licence for two behaviours: both mount the badge, and `at0467` holds a content card in its fixture to keep it that way.

## A Cards row states its place through a window, not the whole run

**A row draws a fixed number of places centred on the one its card holds, whatever the deck's slot count.** It drew the whole imposition once — a chip per slot, the held one lit — which encoded position *geometrically*: find the lit chip in the ruler. That reads well and prices the deck. A chip per place on every row makes a row's width a function of the slot count, so the count becomes the thing that has to stay small, and six already crowded the rail with no answer for eight. The window says position in the **numeral** and keeps the neighbours for the local run, which costs the same at three places as at ten. The count is free to grow again; that is what the change bought, and the reason to prefer it is not the pixels.

**Every chip names a real place, so the window slides rather than overhanging.** It prefers to centre on the card's own slot and gives that up at the ends of the run: a card in the first place draws the first N slots with its own at the left, and a window wider than the arrangement simply draws the arrangement. The first cut held the centre instead and drew the overhang as a dashed **stub** — an empty box at a chip's exact geometry — so the lit chip would land at one offset down the whole column. It looked like damage. The lesson is worth keeping: **an empty position is a fourth slot state nobody can press**, and a reader meeting one tries to press it.

The alignment survives the slide anyway, because the alignment that matters was never the lit chip. The count is the *deck's*, so every row draws the same number of chips and the run's own trailing edge — where the coordinate ends, badge and all — stands on one vertical regardless. What varies per row is which chip is lit, which is the fact being read.

**The card's own chip is the door; every other chip is a move.** Pressing any chip but the card's own puts it there — one press for anywhere the window shows. Pressing the card's own opens the whole arrangement as a popup with that slot filled, because pressing where a card already stands is not a move and the press is better spent on the run: it is how a reader reaches a place the window does not show. That popup is the same surface the card's masthead badge opens, so the gesture is learned once and works from either end. A row whose card stands nowhere — a sidebar card's — has no such chip and gets no door; its window starts at the head of the run, so every chip in it is a live destination.

**A slot run's focus order is the absolute slot, never the drawn position.** Both give the same left-to-right walk, since a window's positions map onto ascending slots — but a focus key is `(group, order)`, and ordering by position means the key a reader stands on names *second chip from the left*. Assigning a slot re-centres the window under them, so that key comes back naming a different place, or none. Keyed by slot, the reader stays on the **place** they were on, which is also the thing they were looking at. `at0278` holds this by moving a card and then requiring the restore to land on the place it moved to.

**The width is five, and it stays switchable.** Three and five are both legible and neither is more correct, so it is a preference rather than a constant — persisted deck-wide through tugbank (`dev.tugapp.slot-window/slotWindow`, never Web storage), read through `useSlotWindow` ([L02]), and set by the `set-slot-window` action.

It had a row on the Layout card for a while and no longer does. That row was the odd one on a card whose every other row states a deck fact: it moved no card and arranged nothing, and so raised no preview layer — there was nothing in a drawing of the deck to audition, and the rows above it redrew themselves the moment it was pressed. Seating it was awkward from both sides: with the deck-wide rows it was plainly a different kind of question, and below them, among rows named for the cards they place — Cards, Jots, Layout — it read as a seventh sidebar card called Slot Window. The width settled at five, so the row went and the switch stayed. **A preference nobody is choosing between does not need a control in the reader's way**; keeping the mechanism costs nothing, and it is what makes taking the control away a reversible decision rather than a deletion.

The window is a projection of `TugSlotLayout`, alongside the run and the scaled map, rather than a second component: same slots, same states, same `setStates`, only what is drawn where changes. States, labels and selection all stay addressed by absolute slot, so a windowed run and a whole one are the same component fed the same facts. Pinned by `at0470`.

## The control cluster is a rollup and a place pair, anchored at its trailing edge

**A pane's title-bar cluster holds every verb behind one `⋯` and pins the place badges against the trailing edge, because that is the cluster's only fixed end.** `.tug-pane-title-bar` is `space-between` with a `flex-shrink: 0` cluster, so the cluster is anchored by its right edge: its *head* is the end that slides whenever one card carries one more verb than its neighbour. The badges led the row for a while on the opposite reasoning, and that produced exactly the defect the reasoning was meant to prevent — a Session card's slot chip and a text card's sat at different x. A readout meant to be taken in at a glance cannot live at the end that moves.

Left to right: the **rollup**, the slot badge, the column badge, the close box. That is the entire cluster. **A verb is either in the rollup or it does not exist** — including the ones a card portals in for itself (on a Session card, the summary trigger and Reveal in Finder, which arrive through a `display: contents` host inside the row). The rule admits no "this one is small" exception: the moment one verb sits outside, the cluster's resting width depends on which card it belongs to again, which is the whole thing the anchored end was for.

**Every control in the cluster is a ghost icon button, badges included, and the row's colour rules address them with no qualification.** The slot badge was the one exception for a while: a bare `TugSlot` control standing among icon buttons, excluded from the cluster's selectors by `:not(.tug-slot)` so its outline would survive them. Buying the chip's own paint that way cost it the row's *behaviour*. Every state the row has had to be re-authored on the chip — rest, hover, pressed, focused pane, background pane — as a copy kept in step by hand, and the hover half of that copy named the outlined-action family: one control in a row of six answered the pointer with an accent rectangle while the other five answered with the row's hover box. The argument that took the accent off the chip at rest — where a card is standing is the deck's resting fact, true whether or not anybody is reaching for it — applies exactly as well under the hand.

So the badge is drawn as a button's **icon** rather than as a control in its own right, and its ink is `currentColor`: the button's, therefore the row's, through every state the row has, with no rule of its own for any of them. The chip keeps its outline by *drawing* one rather than by escaping the cluster's rules. Both badges are cut one step down from the run's 18×22 to 16×20 with the numeral held at 11px, because they stand among 14px glyphs rather than among other chips — at the run's size the pair read as two controls wearing bigger boxes than their neighbours. The general rule this settles: **a badge that stands in a cluster is a member of that cluster**, and a member that needs an exception to the row's rules to look right is telling you it is authored at the wrong altitude. Pinned by `at0462`, which asserts membership structurally — the badge's button matches the selector the row's own rules are written against — rather than by comparing colours, since matching colours is what the hand-kept copy also did.

**Inside the rollup, the shared verbs anchor the trailing end in one order on every kind of card.** Right to left: bullseye, card width, Reveal in Finder. Only the leftmost positions belong to what a particular card adds — Card Settings on a Text card, the session summary on a Session card — because those are different glyphs meaning different things and *those* are what should move between card kinds. A reader who learns where Reveal is on one card finds it at the same offset on the other. Two mechanisms enforce it, because card verbs arrive by two routes: `SHARED_VERB_RANK` in `chrome/tug-pane.tsx` sorts what a card publishes through `paneTitleBarItemsStore`, and a masthead orders its own portal by hand. Pinned by `at0404`.

**Every verb in the rollup is a glyph, and a card's verbs never hide behind a second `⋮` menu.** A `⋮` overflow stood in the row for a while, carrying the Session card's Fold row and its four Go in Transcript rows as a popup. It was cut, and the shape is what is wrong with it rather than its contents: the row itself is already revealed on hover, so a verb inside a menu inside that row is two gestures deep before it is a verb, and a reader who cannot see it has no reason to go looking. `PaneTitleBarItem` therefore has one presentation — a ghost icon button — and a card that wants a verb on its title bar gives it a glyph. A verb with no glyph worth drawing belongs in the native menu bar, where the chord is written beside it anyway.

At rest a card shows a `⋯`, two badges and a close box. The pointer entering the title bar anywhere reveals the verbs, unchanged in glyph, order and act. Four things are load-bearing:

- **The row REPLACES the mark, centre on centre.** Both are anchored to the rollup's trailing edge — the row absolutely, at `inset-inline-end: 0` — so it unfurls leftward from the exact spot the `⋯` occupies and the mark goes out as the row comes in. One control's worth of space, showing one thing at a time; never a row that grows *beside* its own handle. **The row's trailing member is the bullseye target, and the row carries no trailing padding**, so the target's box lands on the mark's box and its centre dot lands on the ellipsis's middle dot: the swap reads as one glyph resolving into another in place. Two pixels of padding there — the first cut had exactly that — shifts the whole set sideways and the eye reads a hop. It is also the right control to put under the dot for a second reason: bullseye is the verb the mark *reports* for, so the thing the dot lights for is the thing the dot becomes.
- **The row is an overlay, not a flex member.** Grown in the flow it would widen the cluster and re-truncate the card's title on every pass of the pointer. As an overlay it moves nothing, and the cluster's measured `--tugx-pane-controls-width` — which the masthead's lower lines inset against — never flinches.
- **Hidden by `opacity` + `pointer-events`, never by `display`.** The buttons keep their boxes, so the unfurl has no layout pass to do and the cluster's real order stays assertable without revealing anything.
- **The reveal reads `data-pointer-within`, written to the bar's DOM from `pointerenter`/`pointerleave`, never React state ([L06]).** Not the `:hover` pseudo-class, and the difference is the point: a pseudo-class is decided by the engine's hit-testing against the physical cursor, which makes a hover-revealed surface unreachable from a test that is not driving a real mouse — and app-tests run in a background window with the user's pointer deliberately untouched. The event pair is what hover is derived from, so a hand gets identical behaviour and a dispatched event reaches the same listener a real one does. Two more clauses complete it: **`data-held`** on the rollup, because a `TugPopupMenu` portals its rows outside the card and the pointer travelling to one would otherwise collapse the row out from under an open menu; and **`:focus-within`**, for a keyboard walk arriving with no pointer at all.

**The `⋯` is a mark, not a button.** The row covers the mark's own spot, so a button there could be pressed to open and never pressed again to close — a control whose second press cannot reach it is worse than none. What it does instead is *report*: a rolled-up control currently holding a posture lights the mark (`data-on`) rather than popping back out into the row, so the fact reaches the surface while the row's membership stays constant, which is the property that makes the cluster learnable. Bullseye is today's only such control.

The cluster has exactly one ellipsis, the horizontal `⋯`, and it means *more of this row*. It was once a pair — a vertical `⋮` beside it for *this card's own menu* — and the pair was learnable but the second glyph's menu was not worth reaching; see the rule above. Pinned by `at0468` and `at0462`.

## The imposer's motion is designed, not assembled

**Every way the imposer moves a card is one of a small set of named operations, each with a stated physics, and no call site picks a curve of its own.** The set lives in `tugdeck/src/lib/imposer-motion.ts`; the table below is what it says.

| Recipe | Operation class | What moves | ζ | Window | Velocity carry |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| `crossing` | a settled arrangement change — the settle | transform + width/height, one curve | 1.0 | 1.0× | from an interrupted tween, else 0 |
| `landing` | a dropped card entering its zone; a refusal's return home | transform | 0.9 | 0.85× | the pointer's release velocity, clamped |
| `reveal` | a strip sliding to show an activated member | the offset property, via the riding frames' crossing | 1.0 | 1.0× | 0 |
| `divide-join` | a member arriving in or leaving a place | opacity (a plain ease; a fade has no position); the survivor moves on `crossing` | — | 0.6× | — |

Two animations in the imposer's files are deliberately outside the family, and both for the same reason — they are not a card being placed. The refusal flash (`flashCardPane`) is a blocked gesture answering back, and the exit ghost is a stand-in div for a Pane that no longer exists, with nowhere to travel to.

Three properties hold the design together:

- **Timing is relative.** Every window is a multiple of the crossing's, which is the deck's one tuning knob (`--tugx-imposer-settle-duration`, read back by `readSettleMs`). A hand on that knob retimes the whole choreography in proportion instead of pulling it apart.
- **The physics rides in the keyframe values, under a `linear` KEYWORD easing** — never a sampled `linear(…)` string, which costs the effect its compositor acceleration. `lib/pane-flip.ts` holds the receipt (18.0% of a core versus 0.9%).
- **A spring is solved in its own time and replayed over the window.** Sizing the spring so it physically settles inside a 360ms window needs ω ≈ 20, and `SpringSolver` integrates forward Euler at a fixed 60Hz — at that frequency the first sampled frame covered 47% of the travel and the damping ratio stopped producing any overshoot at all. The shape is sampled at a well-conditioned frequency and the window sets the tempo.

**An interruption is a velocity-matched retarget, never a snap.** A frame caught mid-settle is cancelled `hold-at-current` — held exactly where the eye has it — and the velocity it was carrying seeds the replacement crossing. The `snap-to-end`-and-restore it replaces committed the tween's final value into inline style and took it back a microtask later, which is long enough to paint: one frame at a stale size against fresh `calc()` geometry.

**A gesture is one commit, so it is one notification.** A release that mutates several times (the autoscroll's offset, the zone, the arriving pane's raise) wraps them in `DeckManager.batchGesture`, because the canvas arms its settle once per notify and an arm landing mid-tween is a retarget nobody asked for. What is batched is observation, not mutation: the writes run in their existing order and lifecycle brackets are untouched.

The numbers are falsifiable rather than felt: `deck-trace`'s motion census counts notifies, settle arms, and retargets per gesture, and a drop-zone release is held to **one notify, at most one arm, and zero snap retargets** (`at0450`, `at0457`).

## Pane-modal vs canvas-overlay surfaces

**A surface that claims pane-modal semantics — "this surface blocks interaction with this pane" — is scoped to the host Pane's stacking context, not to the canvas-overlay tier.** The Pane's outer frame element (`.tug-pane`, exposed via `TugPaneFrameContext` from `tug-pane.tsx`) is its own stacking context: position-absolute with an inline z-index assigned by the deck. Anything portaled into that frame moves with the Pane through drags, resizes and column reflows with no applier and no observer of its own, which is what the frame is for. It is not confined by the frame: `.tug-pane` carries no `overflow`, no `transform` and no `contain`, and `tug-pane.css` forbids adding any, precisely so a portaled panel can paint past the frame's edges when it must.

**Paint order is a separate question from modality, and a Pane holding an open pane-modal surface paints above every peer Pane for as long as that surface is up.** The frame wears `data-sheet-open` while the panel is in the DOM and one stylesheet rule lifts it to `--tug-z-pane-sheet-open` — above every free Pane's focus-order z, below the rail band, the margin caps and the canvas-overlay root, so rails and popups keep their order over it. It is a flat value rather than a rank: the deck's focus-order z-index map is neither read nor written, so focusing another Pane still reorders the rest of the wall exactly as it did. A **rail is exempt** — the rule carries `!important` over the deck's inline z, so on a sidebar Pane, whose inline z is the rail band itself, it would be a drop rather than a lift; a rail already outranks every free Pane, which is the whole of what the lift is for, so a rail holding a panel keeps the band value it has. The lift applies to a panel raised by a store watcher exactly as to one a click opened, and the watcher is the case that needs it — the Pane whose cover is up is typically *not* the focused one, which is the one thing focus order cannot answer. What it trades away is a peer Pane's right to paint over a neighbour's open panel: a pane-modal surface is something the user is being asked to read or answer, and there is no case where the reader wants it partly under something else.

The visual scrim for pane-modal surfaces lives on the Pane: every Pane carries a built-in scrim layer inside its chrome (`.tug-pane-scrim`), default opacity 0. Modal-class consumers raise it via `useTugPaneScrim()`, a ref-counted hook so multiple consumers compose without fighting. The scrim's chrome containment (`.tug-pane-chrome` is its own stacking context via `isolation: isolate`) means the scrim never paints across pane boundaries either.

| Surface class | Portal target | Scope | Hook | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Pane-modal | Pane frame (`TugPaneFrameContext`) | Host pane only | `useTugPaneScrim()` | `TugSheet`, future modal-class surfaces |
| Anchor-relative (transient) | Canvas overlay (`useCanvasOverlay`) | Anchored, may extend past pane | (no scrim) | popovers, tooltips, completion lists |
| App-modal | Canvas overlay (`useCanvasOverlay`) | Whole canvas | (canvas scrim) | `TugAlert` |

The distinction is **the relationship to the host Pane**:

- *"Scoped"* (pane-modal) — the surface blocks interaction with one Pane, paints with the Pane's stacking, moves with the Pane. Portal into the frame.
- *"Anchored"* (transient) — the surface points at a control inside a Pane but may need to extend past the Pane's edges (a popover at the Pane's right edge is allowed to paint over the canvas grid beyond). Portal into canvas overlay.
- *"Whole canvas"* (app-modal) — the surface blocks all interaction across all Panes and the canvas. Portal into canvas overlay.

Modal scope is the Pane stacking context, not the canvas-overlay tier — picking canvas-overlay for a surface that is supposed to be modal-to-one-Pane sets up an entire class of bleed bugs that no amount of measurement-based confinement can fully fix.

The bleed at issue is **interaction** bleed — a surface in one Pane blocking work in another — and every mechanism that prevents it is pane-scoped and independent of where the panel paints. The scrim is `.tug-pane-scrim` inside this Pane's chrome; modality is `inert` on this Pane's `.tug-pane-body`; the focus scope is `loop` without `trapped`, deliberately, because Radix's `trapped` installs a document-global focusin redirect that would yank focus back from other Panes. Paint containment was never one of them. That is why the frame can be lifted above its peers without costing any of the modality this section is about, and it is worth stating because the obvious reading of "scoped to the host Pane's stacking context" is that the stacking is doing the work. It is not.

## Relationship to Other Laws

| Law | Relationship |
|-----|--------------|
| [L09] TugPane composes chrome and owns geometry; Cards never set their own position, size, or z-order | The Pane / Card responsibility split described here. Panes own geometry and chrome; Cards supply content identity |
| [L10] One responsibility per layer | The Pane Model *is* this law applied to the canvas: DeckManager owns the tree, DeckCanvas maps state to Panes, TugPane owns the frame, CardHost owns the content bridge, Card content owns domain logic |
| [L11] Controls emit actions; responders own state | Pane is the responder for Pane-state actions (`close`, `find`, `toggleMenu`). Card is the responder for content-state actions its body implements |
| [L12] Selection stays inside card boundaries | The "card boundary" is the `CardHost` content region — `data-card-id` marks it, `SelectionGuard` clamps at its edges |
| [L23] Internal bookkeeping preserves user-visible state | Cross-Pane moves (detach, merge, reorder) must preserve scroll, selection, focus, and content. This is why `CardHost` portals into the Pane's DOM rather than remounting — React-tree identity is the preservation mechanism |
